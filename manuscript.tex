\date{September 8, 2026}

\begin{abstract}
Does higher education homogenize cultural tastes, or does it preserve and specialize pre-existing aesthetic boundaries? While cross-sectional research links scholastic capital to cultural omnivorousness, how individuals traverse distinct developmental pathways across specific activities during college remains underexplored. Using longitudinal panel data from the NetSense study, we investigate taste trajectories across two cultural domains: leisure book reading (9 genres across six waves) and musical genre preferences (10 genres across six waves) ($N = 201$). We estimate multivariate binary latent class growth models in \texttt{flexmix} using natural cubic splines with endogenous multinomial concomitants. Across both spheres, a four-class specification provides optimal substantive parsimony, distinguishing specialized reading profiles (Genre Specialists, Romance Readers, Nonfictionists, and Fictionists) and musical taste cultures (Omnivores, Classic Rockers, Modern Rockers, and Mainstreamers). Longitudinal analyses show that collegiate cultural change is characterized by targeted specialization and repertoire reallocation rather than wholesale aesthetic drift or boundary decay. In reading, students selectively prune non-core genres over time; in music, Omnivores expand into country, folk, and Broadway while Mainstreamers winnow their repertoires toward univorousness. Endogenous concomitants indicate that pre-collegiate academic capital acts as a sorting gateway into musical omnivorousness, while gender identity, religious affiliation, and undergraduate major channel students into distinct subcultures. Finally, within-class growth models indicate that sociodemographics serve primarily as initial sorting gates into developmental pathways rather than moderating trajectory slopes within them.

\vspace{0.8em}
\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} Cultural Taste, Latent Class Growth Analysis, Cultural Omnivorousness, Higher Education, Emerging Adulthood, Finite Mixture Models
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

The evolution of cultural taste during emerging adulthood represents a central theoretical concern in the sociology of culture and higher education. The collegiate environment is frequently conceptualized as an incubator of aesthetic orientation, providing students with dense social networks, institutional opportunity structures, and exposure to diverse cultural forms. While cross-sectional scholarship has established durable connections between socioeconomic status, scholastic achievement, and cultural omnivorousness \citep{bourdieu1984distinction,peterson1996changing,lizardo2006cultural}, questions remain regarding how individuals traverse distinct developmental trajectories across specific cultural activities over the course of college.

Rather than aggregating participation into summary counts, examining separate binary items simultaneously allows researchers to observe whether taste change is uniform across an entire cultural sphere or driven by specific, institutionalized activities. Using longitudinal survey data from the NetSense study, this investigation models individual developmental pathways across two cultural domains: private leisure reading choices (tracked across 9 distinct book types from freshman through junior year) and musical genre preferences (tracked across 10 prominent musical genres from freshman through junior year). We implement a multivariate binary latent class growth model using natural cubic splines in the \texttt{flexmix} package \citep{grun2008flexmix} in \textsf{R} \citep{Rmanual}. This approach models individual-level trajectories for each item simultaneously while estimating endogenous multinomial concomitants to identify how sociodemographic background, academic achievement, and collegiate major structure latent class membership.

\section{Data and Measures}

\subsection{The NetSense Panel Study}

Empirical analyses use longitudinal survey microdata from the \textit{NetSense} study, a multi-wave panel study tracking a cohort of undergraduate students who matriculated at the University of Notre Dame in August 2011 \citep{striegel2013lessons}. \textit{NetSense} recruited an initial cohort of incoming first-year students, who were provided with sensor-enabled smartphones and surveyed at each semester boundary throughout their undergraduate careers. Surveys captured detailed social networks, academic performance, sociodemographic backgrounds, and extensive inventories of cultural practices. This paper uses the survey data on cultural practices, academic performance and goals, and socio-demographic background.

From the initial survey respondents, two students who had missing data across all survey waves on the cultural inventories were excluded, yielding an analytical cohort of $N = 201$ undergraduate students observed across a total of $1,006$ person-wave observations from freshman matriculation through junior year (Waves 1 through 6, Fall 2011 through Spring 2014). Importantly, cases are not listwise deleted across waves: the longitudinal panel is unbalanced, retaining all available semester observations per student (118 students observed across all 6 waves, 33 across 5 waves, 19 across 4 waves, 8 across 3 waves, 10 across 2 waves, and 13 across 1 wave). In the mixture modeling framework, repeated observations are grouped by student (\texttt{| egoid}), allowing each individual's likelihood contribution to accumulate across their available measurement occasions under Full Information Maximum Likelihood.

\subsection{Longitudinal Cultural Tastes: Books and Music}

Across both cultural domains (music and books), items are measured as binary indicators ($Y_{itj} \in \{0, 1\}$) reflecting semester-level participation or aesthetic preference. For leisure book reading, tracked across Waves 1 through 6, reading choices were measured by survey question Q47: ``\textit{During the past semester, what types of books did you read for pleasure or leisure (not for class assignments)?}'' Binary indicators were recorded across nine literary categories: mysteries and detective fiction; thrillers and suspense; romance; science fiction and fantasy; general and literary fiction; history, politics, and current affairs; biography and memoir; self-help and personal development; and general non-fiction and popular science.

For musical taste preferences, also tracked across Waves 1 through 6, preferences were measured by survey question Q44: ``\textit{Which of the following types of music do you like to listen to?}'' From the full 22-genre inventory administered in the survey, the 10 most popular genres were selected for simultaneous multivariate trajectory modeling: rap and hip-hop; classic rock and oldies; rock and heavy metal; dance music; country; Broadway and show tunes; classical and chamber music; mood and easy listening; folk music; and jazz.

\subsection{Baseline Sociodemographic, Scholastic, and Cultural Covariates}

To explain sorting into latent taste trajectories, we include 15 baseline predictors measured at freshman matriculation, organized into six conceptual blocks. First, demographic identity includes gender identity (1 = Woman, 0 = Man), racial identification (1 = White, 0 = Students of Color), and religious preference (1 = Roman Catholic, 0 = Non-Catholic). Second, family socioeconomic status includes parental household pretax annual income, converted from 14 survey brackets to category midpoints in thousands of dollars (ranging from \$5,000 to \$300,000), alongside parental educational attainment, measured as the maximum years of schooling completed between mother and father (ranging from 10 to 18 years).

Third, pre-collegiate scholastic capital includes high school academic achievement (1 = Mostly A or A- grades, 0 = B+ or lower), advanced degree aspirations (1 = Plans to pursue Master's, Ph.D., M.D., or J.D., 0 = Bachelor's degree only), and Advanced Placement coursework load (1 = Completed 5 or more AP courses, 0 = Under 5 AP courses or not offered). Fourth, secondary schooling habitus captures the institutional and social environment of secondary education, including attendance at a private Catholic or parochial high school (1 = Catholic/parochial, 0 = Public or secular prep) and attendance at a single-sex high school (1 = All-female or all-male academy, 0 = Co-educational).

Fifth, pre-collegiate cultural capital and aesthetic exposure captures embodied cultural practices established prior to university entry, including total arts and cultural event attendance during the year before college (summing across 12 event categories including theater, classical music, dance, museums, and historical sites, ranging from 0 to 12), pre-collegiate leisure reading volume (continuous count of non-assigned books read in the past year, ranging from 0 to 150), and large pre-collegiate music collection size (1 = Owned 1,000 or more tracks at matriculation, 0 = Under 1,000 tracks). Sixth, collegiate and geographic context encompasses declared undergraduate major (1 = Science, Technology, Engineering, or Mathematics, 0 = Arts, Humanities, Social Sciences, or Business) and hometown urbanicity, measured on an ordinal scale ranging from rural farm to major metropolitan area. Missing values on baseline covariates (each under 5\% missingness) were imputed prior to modeling to preserve the full analytical cohort ($N = 201$). \section{Methodological Framework}

\subsection{Multivariate Binary Mixture Formulation}

To model developmental taste pathways across collegiate life, we implement a multivariate binary latent class growth analysis (LCGA) within the finite mixture modeling framework \citep{grun2008flexmix}. Let $i \in \{1, \dots, N\}$ index undergraduate students ($N = 201$) observed across repeated collegiate semesters $t \in T_i \subseteq \{1, \dots, T\}$. At each semester wave, students report binary engagement ($Y_{itj} \in \{0, 1\}$) across an inventory of $J$ distinct cultural items ($J = 9$ for Leisure Book Reading; $J = 10$ for Musical Genre Preferences). We represent student $i$'s complete cultural response history across all observed waves and items as the matrix $\mathbf{Y}_i = \{Y_{itj}\}_{t \in T_i, j \in \{1, \dots, J\}}$.

Rather than forcing all students into a single aggregate trajectory, the finite mixture framework posits that the student population consists of $K$ unobserved latent classes, each defined by a distinct, class-specific trajectory profile across all $J$ items simultaneously. Conditioned on student $i$ belonging to latent class $k \in \{1, \dots, K\}$, item endorsements across categories and waves are modeled as conditionally independent Bernoulli trials governed by time-varying endorsement probabilities $\mu_{iktj} = P(Y_{itj} = 1 \mid c_i = k)$:
\begin{equation}
p(\mathbf{Y}_i \mid c_i = k) = \prod_{t \in T_i} \prod_{j=1}^{J} \mu_{iktj}^{Y_{itj}} (1 - \mu_{iktj})^{1 - Y_{itj}}
\end{equation}
The unconditional marginal likelihood for student $i$ sums over all $K$ latent classes weighted by their prior class probabilities $\pi_{ik} = P(c_i = k)$:
\begin{equation}
p(\mathbf{Y}_i) = \sum_{k=1}^K \pi_{ik} \prod_{t \in T_i} \prod_{j=1}^{J} \mu_{iktj}^{Y_{itj}} (1 - \mu_{iktj})^{1 - Y_{itj}}
\end{equation}
Crucially, repeated observations are grouped at the individual student level. This person-level likelihood accumulation ensures two important analytical properties. First, latent class assignments reflect cohesive, person-level developmental typologies across emerging adulthood rather than fleeting semester-level fluctuations. Second, the product over $t \in T_i$ accommodates unbalanced longitudinal panels through Full Information Maximum Likelihood: students who missed a survey wave contribute full information for all semesters in which they participated ($1,006$ person-wave observations across $201$ students), completely avoiding the statistical inefficiency and potential bias of listwise panel deletion.

Conventional longitudinal growth curve modeling typically assumes either that all individuals follow a single average developmental path (with random individual variations around that average), or evaluates a single summary index---such as a sum of total genres liked or total books read. However, summing across genres collapses qualitatively distinct cultural styles into an abstract number, obscuring the specific aesthetic boundaries that organize social life. In reality, cultural engagement is multidimensional: students do not simply consume ``more'' or ``less'' culture; they assemble specific, cohesive \textit{repertoires} or \textit{bundles} of cultural practices (such as pairing science fiction reading with classic rock and hip-hop listening).

The multivariate binary mixture model acts as an algorithmic pattern-finder across time and cultural items simultaneously. Rather than evaluating whether reading thrillers or listening to rap changes in isolation, the model examines the entire profile of binary checkmarks that each student provides each semester across all items. It asks: \textit{Are there distinct latent classes or developmental corridors that group students who share similar combinations of tastes and similar trajectories of change over their collegiate career?}

Consider a concrete sociological example. Imagine two incoming first-year students who each report liking four musical genres at matriculation. A conventional unidimensional model would view these two students as identical in their cultural breadth. However, the multivariate binary mixture model looks beneath the summary score at the specific bundle of genres endorsed: the first student may listen to rap/hip-hop, dance music, classic rock, and country---a commercial mainstream portfolio. The second student may listen to classical music, jazz, folk, and classic rock---an eclectic, highbrow-leaning portfolio. Over the subsequent six semesters, the model tracks how each student's entire portfolio evolves. It can identify that the first student follows a developmental pathway where rock preferences winnow while country surges to near-universal adoption, while the second student follows an omnivorous pathway where cross-genre diversity is preserved throughout college. By evaluating all items jointly, the model captures cultural lifestyles as integrated developmental systems rather than disconnected, independent choices.

\subsection{Harmonized Natural Spline Time Parameterization}

To achieve temporal flexibility without imposing the rigid symmetry or boundary instability of quadratic polynomials, the probability of endorsing item $j$ at time $t$ in class $k$ is parameterized via an inverse logit link using a natural cubic spline with interior knots placed evenly across the collegiate career at Wave 2 and Wave 4, implemented via the \texttt{splines} package \citep{venables2002modern} in \textsf{R} \citep{Rmanual}:
\begin{equation}
\text{logit}(\mu_{iktj}) = \beta_{0kj} + \beta_{1kj} \text{ns}_1(t) + \beta_{2kj} \text{ns}_2(t) + \beta_{3kj} \text{ns}_3(t)
\end{equation}
where $\text{Time}_{it} = \text{Wave}_{it} - 1$ centers time at collegiate matriculation ($t = 0$), and $\text{ns}_1(t)$, $\text{ns}_2(t)$, and $\text{ns}_3(t)$ represent orthogonalized natural spline basis functions with linear boundary constraints.

Across both cultural domains, students are observed over six consecutive semesters ($t \in \{0, \dots, 5\}$). Boundary knots are placed at $t = 0$ (Wave 1, freshman fall) and $t = 5$ (Wave 6, junior spring), with interior knots placed evenly across the survey waves at Wave 2 ($t = 1$, freshman spring) and Wave 4 ($t = 3$, sophomore spring). Placing knots at these pivotal institutional transitions allows the spline functions to flexibly capture rate adjustments and inflection points corresponding to the completion of first-year general education requirements and second-year major declarations, while preserving linear boundary constraints at matriculation and upperclassman years.

\subsection{Endogenous Multinomial Concomitant Models}

To avoid the classification error and attenuation inherent in two-step estimation, latent class membership probabilities $\pi_{ik} = P(c_i = k \mid \mathbf{X}_i)$ are estimated jointly with item trajectories through an endogenous multinomial logit model:

\begin{equation}
\pi_{ik} = \frac{\exp(\mathbf{X}_i \boldsymbol{\gamma}_k)}{\sum_{l=1}^K \exp(\mathbf{X}_i \boldsymbol{\gamma}_l)}
\end{equation}

where $\boldsymbol{\gamma}_1 = \mathbf{0}$ sets Class 1 as the reference baseline. Across both cultural domains, the expanded battery of 15 baseline predictors is evaluated across six theoretical blocks: demographic identity (gender, race, religion); family socioeconomic status (parental household income and parental education); pre-collegiate scholastic capital (high school academic performance, advanced degree aspirations, and high AP load); secondary schooling habitus (Catholic parochial high school and single-sex schooling); pre-collegiate cultural capital and aesthetic exposure (prior arts event attendance, prior leisure reading volume, and large pre-collegiate music collection); and collegiate and geographic context (declared STEM major and hometown urbanicity).

Across both cultural domains, moving from a single homogeneous growth curve ($K = 1$) to multi-class mixtures produces marked improvements in predictive log-likelihood. In both leisure reading and music preferences, moving from one through four classes yields substantial and continuous reductions in the Bayesian Information Criterion \citep{raftery1995bayesian} ($\text{BIC} = 10465.5$ in Books; $\text{BIC} = 11665.5$ in Music; $p < 0.001$), alongside significant stepwise likelihood ratio tests ($\chi^2 = 333.0, df = 28$ in Books; $\chi^2 = 296.0, df = 31$ in Music). While moving from $K = 4$ to $K = 5$ produces minor statistical gains, it results in small fragmented sub-clusters without substantive gain. Substantively, the four-class solution provides an interpretable sociological typology: in music, it separates specialized classic/traditionalist rock listeners from modern rock and commercial pop listeners, while in reading, it distinguishes cross-genre fiction enthusiasts (Fictionists) from genre-specific sci-fi/thriller specialists, dedicated romance readers, and nonfictionists. Consequently, $K = 4$ is selected as the primary substantive specification across both cultural domains.

The item-specific reading trajectories show that collegiate reading is structured by distinct aesthetic genre specializations rather than an undifferentiated continuum of reading volume. The first class, ``Genre Specialists'' ($n = 63, 31.3\%$), represents the modal plurality: its members concentrate heavily in speculative and narrative fiction including science fiction and fantasy (64.3\% to 69.1\%), other fiction (49.0\% to 50.2\%), and thrillers (33.8\% to 39.9\%), while completely avoiding romance fiction (1.2\% to 2.1\%). The second group, ``Romance Readers'' ($n = 57, 28.4\%$), displays elevated engagement with romance (81.2\% to 93.3\%) and other general fiction (61.5\% to 63.4\%), alongside moderate mystery reading (49.1\% to 61.2\%), but low engagement with history (8.2\% to 16.4\%) or science fiction (38.1\% to 50.9\%). The third group, ``Nonfictionists'' ($n = 42, 20.9\%$), exhibits a dedicated analytical non-fiction profile: its members lead in reading history and politics (77.8\% to 91.2\%), biographies and memoirs (70.6\% to 84.1\%), and other non-fiction (41.2\% to 61.1\%), while reporting low engagement with romance (11.8\% to 14.1\%) and thrillers (7.4\% to 22.8\%). Finally, ``Fictionists'' ($n = 39, 19.4\%$) reads avidly across all fiction categories: science fiction (90.2\% to 97.4\%), thrillers (82.1\% to 91.8\%), mysteries (84.9\% to 90.7\%), and general fiction (53.3\% to 76.1\%), alongside moderate biography engagement (32.8\% to 45.1\%). Across all four classes, reading trajectories remain remarkably stable across all six semesters, demonstrating that literary orientations are established early and persist throughout college.

Examining the item-level music trajectories shows how genre boundaries define distinct student taste cultures. The first class, ``Omnivores'' ($n = 52, 25.9\%$), maintains high preference probabilities across all 10 genres simultaneously, embracing country (77.9\% to 97.0\%), classic rock (73.8\% to 78.4\%), Broadway show tunes (67.2\% to 80.1\%), rap/hip-hop (74.2\% to 77.1\%), dance music (62.1\% to 66.3\%), and classical music (37.1\% to 43.8\%). The second group, ``Classic Rockers'' ($n = 34, 16.9\%$), exhibits an aesthetic blend of rock and traditional highbrow forms: members report near-universal preference for classic rock (76.1\% to 91.1\%) and rock/heavy metal (75.4\% to 92.8\%) alongside elevated classical music (55.6\% to 63.5\%), Broadway (38.9\% to 67.2\%), and jazz (32.9\% to 36.3\%), while actively rejecting country music (9.2\% to 13.4\%) and commercial dance music. The third group, ``Modern Rockers'' ($n = 51, 25.4\%$), combines rock with contemporary commercial rhythms: they listen actively to rock/heavy metal (62.8\% to 80.4\%), rap/hip-hop (75.8\% to 76.5\%), dance music (39.7\% to 56.6\%), and classic rock (44.1\% to 57.7\%), but report almost non-existent tastes for Show Tunes (0.0\% to 3.1\%) and minimal classical listening (8.7\% to 18.9\%). The fourth group, ``Mainstreamers'' ($n = 64, 31.8\%$), concentrates strictly on commercial popular hits: rap/hip-hop (52.1\% to 67.2\%), dance music (37.4\% to 59.2\%), and country (43.1\% to 47.4\%), while almost completely rejecting rock/heavy metal (0.0\% to 20.1\%) or classical music (9.1\% to 18.2\%).

Mainstreamer ``univores'' on the other hand \textit{become} univores by following the reverse pathways, dropping tastes in most genres except for folk.

The block tests provide compelling evidence regarding the social stratification of cultural trajectories. Demographic identity (Gender, Race, and Religion) significantly predicts class sorting across both domains: leisure reading ($\chi^2 = 119.74, df = 9, p < 0.001$) and music preferences ($\chi^2 = 57.39, df = 9, p < 0.001$).\footnote{Although Block~1 is highly significant as a joint demographic set, this aggregate predictive power is driven almost entirely by gender identity and, in leisure reading, religious affiliation. Racial identity (White vs.\ Students of Color) does not significantly predict trajectory class membership in either domain once other baseline covariates are controlled (net multivariable LRT $\chi^2 = 3.38, df = 3, p = 0.336$ in leisure reading; $\chi^2 = 3.70, df = 3, p = 0.296$ in musical preferences; see Table~\ref{tab:app_coefs}). In leisure reading, racial sorting is non-significant even at the bivariate level ($\chi^2 = 3.92, df = 3, p = 0.270$). Because racial identity exhibits no statistically credible net effects in the multivariable models, it is omitted from the marginal probability visualizations in Figure~\ref{fig:marginal} to avoid visual clutter and maintain focus on active sorting mechanisms. Entering college with higher arts exposure—specifically attending dramatic plays, visiting historic landmarks, and attending musical theater—strongly channels students into Omnivores and Classic Rockers while cutting the probability of entering Mainstream or Modern Rock pathways. Furthermore, owning a large personal music library ($\ge 1,000$ tracks) at matriculation significantly insulates students against commercial mainstream sorting ($\text{OR} = 0.34, p = 0.046$; Table~\ref{tab:app_coefs}).

In both domains, scholastic capital (Block 3) acts as a powerful sorting gatekeeper ($\chi^2 = 24.71, df = 9, p = 0.003$ in books; $\chi^2 = 37.69, df = 9, p < 0.001$ in music). Students with heavy AP preparation ($\ge 5$ AP courses) are significantly more likely to sort into Fictionists ($\text{OR} = 1.56$) and Omnivores ($\text{OR} = 4.18, p = 0.025$). Furthermore, secondary schooling habitus (Block 4) leaves a distinct imprint on reading trajectories: students graduating from Catholic parochial high schools are three times more likely to enter Fictionists ($\text{OR} = 3.06, p = 0.047$) and Romance Readers ($\text{OR} = 3.10$) rather than speculative Genre Specialists. Combining scholastic capital with secondary schooling habitus (Combined Educational Habitus, Blocks 3+4) produces a highly significant block across both spheres ($\chi^2 = 32.40, p = 0.006$ in books; $\chi^2 = 41.74, p < 0.001$ in music). Collegiate context (STEM major and hometown urbanicity) also contributes significant sorting in music ($\chi^2 = 14.51, df = 6, p = 0.025$) and books ($\chi^2 = 9.73, df = 6$). In contrast, family socioeconomic status (Block 2: parental income and education) remains completely statistically null across both cultural domains ($\chi^2 = 7.09, p = 0.313$ in books; $\chi^2 = 5.06, p = 0.537$ in music). When all blocks are combined, the full multivariable models achieve decisive statistical significance ($p < 0.001$ in both books and music).

\subsection{Model-Implied Marginal Class Probabilities}

While Table~\ref{tab:blocks} evaluates the overall predictive power of theoretical variable blocks, interpreting individual coefficients from multinomial logit models is often complicated by arbitrary reference baselines and non-linear log-odds metrics. Table~\ref{tab:app_coefs} in the Appendix reports the complete multivariable parameter estimates and odds ratios for all nine covariates across both domains.\footnote{To address sparse-cell and quasi-complete separation issues inherent in categorical choice models with specialized cultural subcultures, multivariable parameters were estimated using penalized maximum likelihood with a mild weight-decay regularization penalty ($\lambda = 0.05$) via the \texttt{nnet} package \citep{venables2002modern} in \textsf{R} \citep{Rmanual}. Specifically, in the leisure reading domain, Romance Readers contains zero male students ($n = 57$ women, $0$ men), and Nonfictionists contains zero students without advanced degree aspirations ($n = 42$ aspirants, $0$ non-aspirants). Under standard unpenalized maximum likelihood estimation, zero cell counts lead to quasi-complete separation \citep{albert1984existence,firth1993bias}, driving the corresponding logit parameters toward the parameter space boundary ($\beta \to \pm\infty$), inflating odds ratios to astronomical figures ($\text{OR} > 10^8$), and producing ill-conditioned covariance matrices with artificially inflated standard errors. Introducing an $L_2$ regularization penalty stabilizes the likelihood surface, preventing numerical divergence while preserving the substantive direction, relative magnitude, and empirical ranking of effects. This penalization ensures that the parameter covariance matrix remains strictly positive definite, yielding well-calibrated, finite simulation confidence intervals and model-implied marginal predicted class probabilities.} Following recent methodological practice in latent growth mixture analysis, we translate these multivariable model parameters into model-implied marginal predicted class probabilities with 95\% simulation confidence intervals, holding other background covariates constant at sample means and reference categories. Prior arts event attendance serves as a primary sorting gateway into musical omnivorousness: students entering with high cultural event attendance (7 events) exhibit a 36.0\% probability of entering Omnivores (compared to 14.5\% for students with low event attendance) and a 22.3\% probability of entering Classic Rockers (vs.\ 9.6\%), while cutting the probability of falling into Mainstreamers (from 43.7\% down to 27.5\%) and Modern Rockers (from 32.3\% down to 14.2\%). Finally, gender identity sharply differentiates rock subcultures: men concentrate heavily in Modern Rockers (45.8\% vs.\ 8.5\% for women) and Classic Rockers (16.8\% vs.\ 9.7\%), whereas women concentrate instead in Mainstreamers (45.3\% vs.\ 22.2\%) and Omnivores (36.6\% vs.\ 15.2\%). Undergraduate major further reinforces these boundaries: declared STEM majors are significantly more likely to sort into Classic Rockers ($\text{OR} = 3.57, p = 0.017$) relative to modern rock and mainstream corridors.

Across both domains, parental socioeconomic status (income and parental education) displays negligible direct sorting power once prior academic achievement and demographic characteristics are controlled. This apparent absence of parental background effects warrants an important substantive and methodological qualification. Rather than indicating that family cultural reproduction has ceased to matter in emerging adulthood, the lack of explanatory power for parental socioeconomic status and cultural capital is likely a consequence of the highly selected nature of the sample. Matriculating at a selective, elite private research university acts as an upstream institutional filter that homogenizes student family backgrounds around high levels of economic and cultural capital. By truncating the variance of parental income and parental education relative to the general population, this institutional selection mechanism attenuates the observed statistical associations between family origin and collegiate cultural sorting, allowing proximate individual characteristics---such as prior academic performance and field of study---to dominate observed taste differentiation on campus.\footnote{Demographic characteristics operate almost exclusively as between-class sorting gates (the extensive margin) rather than within-class slope modifiers (the intensive margin). To test whether baseline characteristics moderate the rate and shape of change within established pathways, we estimated class-stratified multilevel mixed-effects logistic models using the \texttt{lme4} package \citep{bates2015fitting} in \textsf{R} \citep{Rmanual} across both domains, entering individual random intercepts and evaluating interactions between the time spline basis and focal demographic predictors ($\text{Time} \times \text{Woman}$ and $\text{Time} \times \text{STEM Major}$). Across all strata, within-class demographic slope moderation is statistically null ($p > 0.10$). Once students enter a given developmental corridor, they share a common institutional habitus, experiencing stable trajectory dynamics regardless of their demographic background.}

\section{Discussion}

\subsection{Summary of Key Results}

This study implemented a growth mixture framework with natural cubic splines and endogenous multinomial concomitants to model longitudinal cultural taste trajectories across emerging adulthood in the NetSense study. By estimating item-specific growth parameters across 9 book reading genres and 10 musical genres across all six collegiate semesters, our analysis uncovers three core substantive insights into collegiate cultural dynamics.

First, item-level modeling shows that leisure reading and musical preferences divide students into persistent genre specializations that remain stable across all six semesters rather than undergoing wholesale aesthetic drift. Second, within-class moderation models show that demographic background and collegiate majors act as sorting gates into broad developmental pathways rather than moderating trajectory slopes within those pathways. Third, endogenous concomitant models show that sorting into these cultural pathways is driven by gender identity, pre-collegiate cultural capital, scholastic capital, and secondary schooling habitus rather than parental socioeconomic resources. Pre-collegiate cultural capital acts as a primary sorting gateway into musical omnivorousness: students arriving with extensive arts exposure and large music collections are overwhelmingly channeled into omnivorous and classic rock pathways while avoiding commercial mainstream univorousness. Concurrently, secondary schooling habitus and scholastic capital channel students into distinct literary spheres: attending Catholic parochial high schools and completing rigorous AP loads triply increases the odds of sorting into avid fiction reading corridors. Meanwhile, gender identity reinforces enduring cultural boundaries, sorting women into Romance Reading and Mainstream musical tastes while channeling STEM majors into classic rock.

\subsection{Limitations and Suggestions for Future Work}

Several analytical considerations should guide the interpretation of these findings. First, regarding operationalization and measurement constraints, the self-reported binary batteries analyzed here record whether an individual engaged in a cultural activity or endorsed a musical genre during a given semester, but do not capture the intensive margin of engagement, such as the repeated frequency of attendance, financial expenditures, or the subjective depth of aesthetic experience. Furthermore, discrete item batteries are bounded by fixed survey inventories that omit emerging, decentralized digital cultural forms. Future research would benefit from pairing longitudinal survey instruments with multimodal digital trace data, such as streaming platform logs, electronic ticketing records, or mobile reading tracking applications, to capture the continuous volume and affective valence of cultural encounters.

Second, regarding threats to causal inference and contextual confounding, our observational design models endogenous sorting into developmental pathways rather than exogenous causal interventions. However, by directly incorporating pre-collegiate cultural capital (prior arts event attendance, leisure reading volume, and music library size) alongside secondary schooling habitus and scholastic capital at matriculation, our specification substantially tightens causal bounds by controlling for pre-existing behavioral momentum. Unmeasured contextual factors, such as campus-subsidized cultural excursions, department-specific course reading requirements, or variations in financial aid packages that alter disposable leisure income, could still influence within-college taste evolution. Future studies could leverage natural experiments, such as randomized housing assignments or lottery-based course enrollments, to isolate institutional treatment effects from pre-existing student self-selection.

Third, regarding directionality, selection, and reciprocal feedback loops, cultural taste trajectories likely co-evolve alongside interpersonal social networks and collegiate friendship circles. Students may select peers who share similar aesthetic preferences, while peer networks simultaneously reinforce or broaden existing cultural repertoires through social contagion. Our person-level latent class formulation treats class membership as an enduring person-level type rather than modeling bidirectional dynamic feedback over time. Future investigations should integrate multivariate mixture models with continuous-time stochastic actor-oriented models or cross-lagged panel models to unpack how peer tie formation and taste diversification dynamically influence each other across the collegiate life course.

Fourth, regarding temporal granularity and panel attrition, our panel tracks participants across semester-length intervals over three full collegiate years. While semester-spaced waves effectively capture macro-level institutional rhythms, such as transitioning between academic years and declaring majors, they cannot detect short-term micro-churn in media consumption or fleeting aesthetic fads. Moreover, although our analytic samples were restricted to complete-case respondents with high longitudinal compliance, panel studies over multiple collegiate years remain vulnerable to selective attrition, particularly among students experiencing academic distress or social isolation. Implementing ecological momentary assessment (EMA) or frequent micro-surveys would enable researchers to observe micro-level taste shifts while tracking non-response dynamics across extended developmental windows.

Fifth, regarding institutional scope conditions and generalizability, the NetSense cohort is situated within a single selective, private, highly residential research university with a distinctive collegiate culture and a predominantly White (67.8\%) and high-SES student body. The institutional opportunity structures present on this campus---including active campus performing arts centers, subsidized student programming, and a strong residential hall system---differ markedly from those found in large public institutions, commuter campuses, or community colleges. 

Crucially, the highly selected nature of this elite undergraduate sample provides the most plausible explanation for the observed lack of explanatory power for parental socioeconomic background. Highly selective university admissions serve as an upstream institutional filter that disproportionately admits students from affluent, highly educated households with high baseline endowments of economic and cultural capital. By severely compressing the variance of parental income and parental education relative to the general population, this institutional selection mechanism creates a restriction-of-range problem that attenuates the observed statistical associations between family origin and collegiate cultural sorting. Rather than showing that class reproduction has dissolved in emerging adulthood, our findings demonstrate that once students pass through the elite admissions threshold, proximate embodied factors---specifically pre-collegiate cultural capital, secondary schooling habitus, and pre-collegiate scholastic capital---supersede abstract family wealth as the primary visible sorting channels. Comparative research across diverse organizational settings, regional ecologies, and less selective higher education institutions will be essential to test whether parental socioeconomic status re-emerges as a decisive sorting mechanism in more socioeconomically heterogeneous student bodies.

\subsection{Implications: Repertoires, Institutions, and the Collegiate Habitus}

These empirical findings contribute directly to sociological theories of cultural capital, omnivorousness, and life course transitions \citep{bourdieu1984distinction}. Our results indicate that higher education does not homogenize student aesthetic repertoires into a shared collegiate taste culture. Instead, students enter higher education with distinct, specialized aesthetic dispositions that remain durable across three full years of undergraduate education.

Furthermore, our findings illuminate the mechanisms through which the collegiate habitus operates. Rather than functioning as a blank slate or an engine of cultural conversion, elite higher education functions as an institutional sorting machine that recognizes, amplifies, and channels accumulated secondary habitus. Students arriving with rich pre-collegiate cultural capital and rigorous secondary schooling do not discard their tastes; instead, they deploy them into high-status omnivorous pathways that confer distinction within collegiate peer circles. Cultural omnivorousness does not imply that individuals consume all cultural genres equally; rather, it reflects structured combinations of highbrow traditionalism, commercial popular culture, and specialized leisure reading. When activities impose high logistical and physical coordination costs---as with attending theater performances and concerts---students navigate institutional time binds through selective participation slumps. In contrast, private consumption practices---such as reading fiction or non-fiction---remain insulated from institutional rhythms. Recognizing these domain-specific and item-specific dynamics enriches our understanding of how cultural capital operates during emerging adulthood.
