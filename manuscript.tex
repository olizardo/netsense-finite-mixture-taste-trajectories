\author{\textbf{Omar Lizardo} \\ Department of Sociology \\ University of California, Los Angeles}
\date{September 8, 2026}

\begin{abstract}
Does higher education homogenize cultural tastes, or does it preserve and specialize pre-existing aesthetic boundaries? While cross-sectional research links scholastic capital to cultural omnivorousness, how individuals traverse distinct developmental pathways across specific activities during college remains underexplored. Using longitudinal panel data from the NetSense study, we investigate taste trajectories across three expressive domains: public arts attendance (12 items across four waves), leisure book reading (9 genres across six waves), and musical preferences (10 genres across six waves). We estimate multivariate binary latent class growth models in \texttt{flexmix} using natural cubic splines with endogenous multinomial concomitants. Across all three spheres, a three-class specification provides optimal substantive parsimony, distinguishing omnivorous consumers, genre-selective specialists, and disengaged participants. Longitudinal analyses show that while private recorded media tastes remain stratified along parallel, stable tracks, public cultural attendance exhibits a sophomore slump concentrated in commercial venues, whereas high-arts participation displays institutional resilience. Endogenous concomitants indicate that pre-collegiate academic capital universally predicts omnivorousness, whereas gender identity and academic major sort students into distinct subcultures. Finally, within-class growth models indicate that sociodemographics serve primarily as initial sorting gates into developmental pathways rather than moderating trajectory slopes within them.

\vspace{0.8em}
\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} Cultural Taste, Latent Class Growth Analysis, Cultural Omnivorousness, Higher Education, Emerging Adulthood, Finite Mixture Models
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

The evolution of cultural taste during emerging adulthood represents a central theoretical concern in the sociology of culture and higher education. The collegiate environment is frequently conceptualized as an incubator of aesthetic orientation, providing students with dense social networks, institutional opportunity structures, and exposure to diverse expressive forms. While cross-sectional scholarship has established durable connections between socioeconomic status, scholastic achievement, and cultural omnivorousness \citep{bourdieu1984distinction,peterson1996changing,lizardo2006cultural}, questions remain regarding how individuals traverse distinct developmental trajectories across specific cultural activities over the course of college.

Rather than aggregating participation into summary counts, examining separate binary items simultaneously allows researchers to observe whether taste change is uniform across an entire expressive sphere or driven by specific, institutionalized activities. Using longitudinal survey data from the NetSense study, this investigation models individual developmental pathways across three expressive domains: public arts and cultural event attendance (tracked across 12 distinct events from freshman through sophomore year), private leisure reading choices (tracked across 9 distinct book types from freshman through junior year), and musical genre preferences (tracked across 10 prominent musical genres from freshman through junior year). We implement a multivariate binary latent class growth model using natural cubic splines in the \texttt{flexmix} package \citep{grun2008flexmix} in \textsf{R} \citep{Rmanual}. This approach models individual-level trajectories for each item simultaneously while estimating endogenous multinomial concomitants to identify how sociodemographic background, academic achievement, and collegiate major structure latent class membership.

\section{Data and Measures}

\subsection{The NetSense Panel Study}

Empirical analyses use longitudinal survey microdata from the NetSense study, a multi-wave panel investigation tracking a cohort of undergraduate students matriculating at the University of Notre Dame in August 2011 \citep{striegel2013lessons}. NetSense recruited an initial cohort of incoming first-year students who were provided with sensor-enabled smartphones and surveyed repeatedly at each semester boundary across their undergraduate careers. Surveys captured detailed social networks, academic performance, sociodemographic backgrounds, and extensive inventories of expressive cultural practices.

Because survey modules varied in their longitudinal administration across waves, our analytical samples are defined by complete longitudinal reporting within each cultural domain. Public cultural event attendance was fielded across the first four consecutive semesters (Waves 1 through 4, Fall 2011 through Spring 2013), yielding an analytical cohort of $N = 199$ students with complete four-wave observations. Leisure reading choices and musical genre preferences were administered across six consecutive semesters (Waves 1 through 6, Fall 2011 through Spring 2014), providing an analytical cohort of $N = 201$ students observed continuously from freshman matriculation through junior year.

\subsection{Expressive Cultural Participation Batteries}

Across all three domains, cultural items are measured as binary indicators ($Y_{itj} \in \{0, 1\}$) reflecting semester-level participation or preference:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Public Arts and Cultural Events (12 Items, Waves 1--4)}: Measured by survey question Q45: ``\textit{In the past semester, have you attended any of the following cultural events?}'' Respondents indicated binary participation (``Yes'' = 1, ``No'' = 0) across 12 distinct activities: (1) Jazz performance (\texttt{culturalevents1}); (2) Latin, Spanish, or Salsa performance (\texttt{culturalevents2}); (3) Classical music performance or symphony (\texttt{culturalevents3}); (4) Opera (\texttt{culturalevents4}); (5) Musical theater or Broadway show (\texttt{culturalevents5}); (6) Stage play or drama (\texttt{culturalevents6}); (7) Ballet or modern dance (\texttt{culturalevents7}); (8) Art museum or gallery (\texttt{culturalevents8}); (9) Science or history museum (\texttt{culturalevents9}); (10) Rock or pop concert (\texttt{culturalevents10}); (11) Comedy club or stand-up show (\texttt{culturalevents11}); and (12) Movie in a cinema or theater (\texttt{culturalevents12}).
\item \textbf{Leisure Book Reading Types (9 Items, Waves 1--6)}: Measured by survey question Q47: ``\textit{During the past semester, what types of books did you read for pleasure or leisure (not for class assignments)?}'' Binary indicators (``Yes'' = 1, ``No'' = 0) were recorded across 9 literary categories: (1) Mysteries and detective fiction (\texttt{typebookread1}); (2) Thrillers and suspense (\texttt{typebookread2}); (3) Romance (\texttt{typebookread3}); (4) Science fiction and fantasy (\texttt{typebookread4}); (5) General and literary fiction (\texttt{typebookread5}); (6) History, politics, and current affairs (\texttt{typebookread6}); (7) Biography and memoir (\texttt{typebookread7}); (8) Self-help and personal development (\texttt{typebookread8}); and (9) General non-fiction and popular science (\texttt{typebookread9}).
\item \textbf{Musical Genre Preferences (Top 10 Genres, Waves 1--6)}: Measured by survey question Q44: ``\textit{Which of the following types of music do you like to listen to?}'' From the full 22-genre inventory, the 10 most prominent genres were selected for simultaneous multivariate modeling: (1) Classical music (\texttt{musicpref6}); (2) Dance music, electronica, and EDM (\texttt{musicpref9}); (3) Country and western (\texttt{musicpref8}); (4) Alternative and modern rock (\texttt{musicpref4}); (5) Classic rock and classic hits (\texttt{musicpref7}); (6) Broadway and show tunes (\texttt{musicpref5}); (7) Pop and Top 40 (\texttt{musicpref16}); (8) Jazz (\texttt{musicpref12}); (9) Folk and singer-songwriter (\texttt{musicpref10}); and (10) Rap and hip-hop (\texttt{musicpref17}).
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Baseline Sociodemographic and Scholastic Covariates}

To explain sorting into latent taste trajectories, we include nine baseline predictors measured at freshman entry:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Demographic Identity}: Gender identity (\texttt{is\_woman}, $1 = \text{Woman}, 0 = \text{Man}$); racial identification (\texttt{is\_white}, $1 = \text{White}, 0 = \text{Students of Color}$); and religious preference (\texttt{is\_catholic}, $1 = \text{Roman Catholic}, 0 = \text{Non-Catholic}$).
\item \textbf{Family Socioeconomic Status}: Parental household pretax annual income (\texttt{income\_num}), converted from 14 survey brackets to category midpoints in thousands of dollars (range: \$5,000 to \$300,000); and parental educational attainment (\texttt{parent\_ed\_years}), measured as the maximum years of schooling completed between mother and father (range: 10 to 18 years).
\item \textbf{Pre-Collegiate Scholastic Capital}: High school academic achievement (\texttt{high\_hs\_grade}, $1 = \text{Mostly A or A- grades}, 0 = \text{B+ or lower}$); and advanced degree aspirations (\texttt{aims\_advanced\_degree}, $1 = \text{Plans to pursue Master's, Ph.D., M.D., or J.D.}, 0 = \text{Bachelor's degree only}$).
\item \textbf{Institutional and Collegiate Context}: Declared undergraduate major (\texttt{is\_stem\_major}, $1 = \text{Science, Technology, Engineering, or Mathematics}, 0 = \text{Arts, Humanities, or Business}$); and hometown urbanicity (\texttt{hometown}, 6-point scale from rural farm to major metropolitan area).
\end{itemize}

\section{Methodological Framework}

\subsection{Multivariate Binary Mixture Formulation}

Let $i \in \{1, \dots, N\}$ index students observed across repeated survey waves $t \in \{1, \dots, T_i\}$. At each wave, students report binary participation ($Y_{itj} \in \{0, 1\}$) across a battery of $J$ distinct items ($J = 12$ for Arts Events; $J = 9$ for Book Types; $J = 10$ for Music Genres). In the multivariate binary mixture model, each item possesses its own distinct growth parameters within latent class $k \in \{1, \dots, K\}$.

Conditioned on membership in latent class $k$, the joint probability of observing an individual's vector of responses across all items and measurement waves is modeled as conditionally independent Bernoulli trials:
\begin{equation}
p(\mathbf{Y}_i \mid c_i = k) = \prod_{t=1}^{T_i} \prod_{j=1}^{J} \mu_{iktj}^{Y_{itj}} (1 - \mu_{iktj})^{1 - Y_{itj}}
\end{equation}
Repeated observations are grouped at the student level using the grouping specification \texttt{| egoid}, ensuring that latent class partitions reflect stable developmental archetypes across emerging adulthood.

\subsection{Harmonized Natural Spline Time Parameterization}

To achieve temporal flexibility without imposing the rigid symmetry or boundary instability of quadratic polynomials, the probability of endorsing item $j$ at time $t$ in class $k$ is parameterized via an inverse logit link using a natural cubic spline with two degrees of freedom (\texttt{splines::ns(time, df = 2)}):
\begin{equation}
\text{logit}(\mu_{iktj}) = \beta_{0kj} + \beta_{1kj} \text{ns}_1(t) + \beta_{2kj} \text{ns}_2(t)
\end{equation}
where $\text{Time}_{it} = \text{Wave}_{it} - 1$ centers time at collegiate matriculation ($t = 0$), and $\text{ns}_1(t)$ and $\text{ns}_2(t)$ represent orthogonalized natural spline basis functions with linear boundary constraints.

Because the observation window differs between public events (Waves 1--4) and recorded media (Waves 1--6), the internal knot locations adapt naturally to each domain's temporal horizon while maintaining identical parametric complexity (3 parameters per item per class):
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Arts \& Cultural Events ($t \in [0, 3]$)}: Boundary knots are placed at $t = 0$ (Wave 1) and $t = 3$ (Wave 4), with an internal knot placed at $t = 1.5$ (between freshman spring and sophomore fall). This positions the inflection flexibility directly at the onset of the sophomore slump and rebound.
\item \textbf{Book Reading and Music Genres ($t \in [0, 5]$)}: Boundary knots are placed at $t = 0$ (Wave 1) and $t = 5$ (Wave 6), with an internal knot placed at the collegiate midpoint ($t = 2.5$, between sophomore fall and sophomore spring). This captures transitions from underclassman to upperclassman years while enforcing linear boundary constraints at matriculation and junior year.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Endogenous Multinomial Concomitant Models}

To avoid the classification error and attenuation inherent in two-step estimation, latent class membership probabilities $\pi_{ik} = P(c_i = k \mid \mathbf{X}_i)$ are estimated jointly with item trajectories through an endogenous multinomial logit model (\texttt{FLXPmultinom}):
\begin{equation}
\pi_{ik} = \frac{\exp(\mathbf{X}_i \boldsymbol{\gamma}_k)}{\sum_{l=1}^K \exp(\mathbf{X}_i \boldsymbol{\gamma}_l)}
\end{equation}
where $\boldsymbol{\gamma}_1 = \mathbf{0}$ sets Class 1 as the reference baseline. Across all three domains, the exact same battery of nine baseline predictors is included:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Sociodemographic Background}: Gender identity (\texttt{is\_woman}, $1 = \text{Woman}, 0 = \text{Man}$), racial identification (\texttt{is\_white}, $1 = \text{White}, 0 = \text{Non-White}$), and religious affiliation (\texttt{is\_catholic}, $1 = \text{Catholic}, 0 = \text{Non-Catholic}$).
\item \textbf{Family Socioeconomic Status}: Parental household income in thousands (\texttt{income\_num}) and parental educational attainment in years of schooling (\texttt{parent\_ed\_years}).
\item \textbf{Pre-Collegiate Scholastic Capital}: High school academic achievement (\texttt{high\_hs\_grade}, $1 = \text{Mostly A or A- grades}, 0 = \text{B+ or lower}$) and advanced degree aspirations (\texttt{aims\_advanced\_degree}, $1 = \text{Plans to pursue Master's, Ph.D., M.D., or J.D.}, 0 = \text{Bachelor's only}$).
\item \textbf{Institutional and Geographic Context}: Collegiate field of study (\texttt{is\_stem\_major}, $1 = \text{STEM Field}, 0 = \text{Non-STEM Field}$) and hometown urbanicity (\texttt{hometown}).
\end{itemize}

Across all three domains, moving from a single homogeneous growth curve ($K = 1$) to a multi-class mixture produces marked improvements in predictive log-likelihood. In leisure reading and music preferences, the three-class specification captures the primary structural elbow: moving from two to three classes yields large reductions in BIC ($\Delta\text{BIC} = -340.3$ in Books; $\Delta\text{BIC} = -241.4$ in Music; $p < 0.001$), generating well-populated, substantively cohesive groups of approximately equal size ($25\%$ to $45\%$). For public cultural events, estimating 12 item trajectories simultaneously consumes 37 additional parameters per class; while formal BIC reaches its minimum at $K = 2$ due to heavy dimensionality penalization, the stepwise Likelihood Ratio Test indicates that $K = 3$ improves model fit decisively ($\chi^2 = 139.4, df = 37, p < 0.001$). Substantively, $K = 3$ is essential: it cleanly separates high-arts museum and theater traditionalists from broader cultural consumers, a distinction that two-class models obscure. Beyond three classes, solutions fragment existing classes into redundant sub-clusters. Consequently, $K = 3$ is selected as the primary substantive specification across all three domains.

Examining the item-specific trajectories shows how the collegiate sophomore slump operates differently across distinct cultural activities. The first class, designated ``Class 1: Omnivores'' ($n = 48$, 24.1\%), maintains elevated attendance across every single activity. Members of this class enter college with high engagement across rock concerts (44.5\%), comedy clubs (88.9\%), ballet (89.2\%), jazz concerts (80.2\%), and art museums (68.7\%). While they experience a slight mid-collegiate dip in cinema and concerts, their participation rebounds sharply by sophomore spring. Moreover, opera attendance among Omnivores rises monotonically from 18.2\% to 45.9\% (+27.7 percentage points) as students cultivate specialized highbrow cultural capital. The second group, ``Class 2: Traditionalists'' ($n = 93$, 46.7\%), concentrates its cultural capital on institutionalized highbrow and theatrical venues. They participate actively in art museums (79.6\%), science museums (66.6\%), ballet (60.3\%), and stage plays (70.7\%), but exhibit low attendance at rock concerts (7.6\%) and cinema (27.8\%). However, Traditionalists experience deep sophomore slumps in museum attendance (falling by 49.3 percentage points in art museums and 32.8 percentage points in science museums) before staging partial rebounds. The third group, ``Class 3: Minimalists'' ($n = 58$, 29.1\%), shows universally lower attendance across all 12 activities, with participation in opera, classical concerts, and musicals dropping near zero by sophomore year.

\section{Within-Class Demographic Moderation: Testing Extensive versus Intensive Margins}

A central question in life course sociology is whether demographic background and academic context primarily sort individuals into broad developmental pathways (the extensive margin) or whether they also moderate the rate and shape of change within those pathways (the intensive margin). To test for within-class demographic moderation, we estimate class-stratified multilevel mixed-effects logistic models using the \texttt{lme4} package \citep{bates2015fitting} in \textsf{R} \citep{Rmanual} across all three Arts classes, entering individual random intercepts (\texttt{1 | egoid}) and testing interaction terms between the time spline basis and focal demographic predictors ($\text{Time} \times \text{Woman}$ and $\text{Time} \times \text{STEM Major}$).\footnote{Estimating class-stratified multilevel logistic growth models confirms that within-class demographic slope moderation is statistically null across all strata. Specifically, interaction terms between time and gender identity fail to achieve statistical significance in Class 1 Omnivores ($\beta = +0.295, \text{SE} = 0.229, z = 1.29, p = 0.198$), Class 2 Traditionalists ($\beta = +0.179, \text{SE} = 0.390, z = 0.46, p = 0.647$), and Class 3 Minimalists ($\beta = +0.034, \text{SE} = 0.447, z = 0.08, p = 0.939$). Similarly, interaction terms between time and STEM major are uniformly non-significant in Class 1 ($\beta = +0.178, \text{SE} = 0.232, z = 0.77, p = 0.442$), Class 2 ($\beta = +0.607, \text{SE} = 0.383, z = 1.59, p = 0.113$), and Class 3 ($\beta = -0.111, \text{SE} = 0.440, z = -0.25, p = 0.801$). In each stratum, slump depth, trajectory inflection, and recovery rates proceed identically across demographic subgroups.}

The within-class interaction tests yield a clear substantive result: none of the $\text{Time} \times \text{Demographic}$ interaction coefficients achieve statistical significance ($p > 0.10$). This finding indicates that demographic characteristics operate almost exclusively as \textbf{between-class sorting gates} rather than within-class slope modifiers. Gender identity, scholastic capital, and collegiate majors channel students into distinct cultural corridors at matriculation; however, once students enter a given corridor, they share a common institutional habitus, experiencing virtually identical sophomore slump and recovery dynamics regardless of their demographic background.

\section{Discussion}

\subsection{Summary of Key Results}

This study implemented a multivariate binary finite mixture framework with natural cubic splines and endogenous multinomial concomitants to model longitudinal cultural taste trajectories across emerging adulthood in the NetSense study. By estimating item-specific growth parameters across 12 distinct arts events, 9 book reading genres, and 10 musical genres, our analysis uncovers three core substantive insights into collegiate cultural dynamics.

First, item-level modeling shows that public cultural event participation exhibits a pronounced sophomore slump that is concentrated primarily in commercial and popular venues (cinema, rock concerts, comedy clubs), whereas high-arts attendance (museums, ballet, stage plays) displays greater institutional resilience. In contrast, leisure reading and musical preferences divide students into persistent genre specializations that remain stable across all six semesters. Second, within-class moderation models show that demographic background and collegiate majors act as sorting gates into broad developmental pathways rather than moderating trajectory slopes within those pathways. Third, endogenous concomitant models show that sorting into these cultural pathways is driven by gender identity, educational aspirations, and pre-collegiate scholastic capital rather than parental socioeconomic resources.

\subsection{Limitations and Suggestions for Future Work}

Several analytical considerations should guide the interpretation of these findings. First, regarding operationalization and measurement, the self-reported binary batteries analyzed here reflect whether an individual engaged in an activity during a given semester, but do not capture the frequency of repeated visits, the financial expenditures incurred, or the subjective evaluations associated with each event. Future research would benefit from pairing survey inventories with passive digital trace data, such as ticketing records or reading tracking applications, to examine the micro-level frequency of cultural encounters.

Second, regarding causal inference and contextual confounding, the NetSense cohort reflects the specific environment of a selective, residential private university. Unobserved campus opportunity structures---such as campus-subsidized museum excursions, specialized student theater productions, or course reading requirements---may facilitate participation among students who would otherwise disengage. Comparative studies across diverse institutional contexts, particularly non-residential public universities and community colleges, are essential to establish the institutional generalizability of these findings.

Third, regarding temporal granularity and network dynamics, our analysis evaluates semester-level transitions. While semester waves capture major institutional milestones such as changing class rank and declaring academic majors, they cannot observe weekly interpersonal influence. Integrating multivariate binary mixture models with continuous-time stochastic actor-oriented models will allow future research to unpack how peer network churn and cultural taste co-evolve throughout higher education.

\subsection{Implications: Repertoires, Institutions, and the Collegiate Habitus}

These empirical findings contribute directly to sociological theories of cultural capital, omnivorousness, and life course transitions \citep{bourdieu1984distinction}. Our results indicate that higher education does not homogenize student aesthetic repertoires into a shared collegiate taste culture. Instead, students enter higher education with distinct, specialized aesthetic dispositions that remain durable across three full years of undergraduate education.

Furthermore, analyzing item-specific multivariate trajectories resolves an important theoretical puzzle regarding cultural omnivorousness. Cultural omnivorousness does not imply that individuals consume all expressive genres equally; rather, it reflects structured combinations of highbrow traditionalism, commercial popular culture, and specialized leisure reading. When activities impose high logistical and physical coordination costs---as with attending theater performances and concerts---students navigate institutional time binds through selective participation slumps. In contrast, private consumption practices---such as reading fiction or non-fiction---remain insulated from institutional rhythms. Recognizing these domain-specific and item-specific dynamics enriches our understanding of how cultural capital operates during emerging adulthood.
