\title{\textbf{Uncovering Latent Trajectories of Cultural Taste During the Transition to College}}
\author{
  Omar Lizardo\thanks{Department of Sociology, University of California, Los Angeles. Email: \texttt{olizardo@soc.ucla.edu}.}}
\date{September 8, 2026}

\begin{abstract}
Does higher education homogenize cultural tastes, or does it preserve and specialize pre-existing aesthetic boundaries? While cross-sectional research links scholastic capital to cultural omnivorousness, how individuals traverse distinct cultural taste trajectories during college remains underexplored. Using longitudinal panel data from the \textit{NetSense} study, this paper investigates taste trajectories across two cultural domains: private leisure reading (9 genres) and musical preferences (10 genres) across six semesters ($N = 201$, $1,006$ person-wave observations). I estimate multivariate binary latent class growth models in \texttt{flexmix} parameterized via quadratic polynomial growth curves with endogenous multinomial concomitants. In each sphere, four distinct cultural taste trajectories emerge: in leisure reading, students sort into Genre Specialists ($37.3\%$), Romance Readers ($23.4\%$), Fictionists ($22.9\%$), and Nonfictionists ($16.4\%$); in musical taste, students sort into Mainstreamers ($30.8\%$), Modern Rockers ($26.4\%$), Omnivores ($24.4\%$), and Classic Rockers ($18.4\%$). Trajectory analyses reveal marked domain asymmetry: leisure reading exhibits selective winnowing and specialization under academic time scarcity, whereas musical taste displays substantial aesthetic expansion among Omnivores alongside consolidation among Mainstreamers. Endogenous concomitant models show that sorting into trajectories is driven by pre-collegiate cultural capital (prior arts event attendance and personal music library scale), scholastic capital (high school GPA and AP coursework), secondary schooling habitus, and gender identity, whereas parental income and education are statistically null. Finally, multilevel growth models show that baseline attributes operate strictly as extensive-margin sorting gates rather than intensive-margin slope moderators. Collegiate cultural stratification thus reflects accumulated habitus and behavioral path-dependency rather than family wealth.

\vspace{0.8em}
\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} Cultural Taste, Latent Class Growth Analysis, Cultural Omnivorousness, Cultural Capital, Higher Education, Emerging Adulthood, Finite Mixture Models
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Ever since Richard Peterson introduced the felicitous term ``patterns of cultural choice'' into sociological discourse more than four decades ago \citep{peterson1983patterns-f85}, sociologists have been busy empirically uncovering and debating the sociological reality, drivers, meaning, and correlates of various such patterns. These include, most famously and influentially, the Petersonian cultural \textit{omnivore} \citep{peterson1996changing-967, goldberg2011mapping-77a, warde2008omnivorous-e77} along with their evil twin the culturally exclusive \textit{univore} \citep{bryson1997what-933, peterson1992understanding-ce3, tonkovi2025globallocal-96f}, but also culturally \textit{voracious} consumers \citep{sullivan2007omnivore-018}, \textit{poly-purists} and \textit{poly-mixers} \citep{goldberg2016what-4e3, poecke2018pure-873}, \textit{decisives} and \textit{indecisives} \citep{sonnett2004musical-961}, and even plain old \textit{snobs} engaging in (presumably) Bourdieusian boundary-drawing distinction \citep{atkinson2011context-d44, nault2021social-1bf}, among many others.

Yet, less work has been done on the presumed \textit{origins} of such patterns. A preponderant reason for the relative silence of the literature on this question is that, for the most part, the workhorse sources of data for most sociological work on tastes---publicly funded arts participation surveys or customized social science surveys---are populated mostly by middle-aged, older, and senior adults whose patterns of tastes, given what we know about how early formative experiences are paramount---are probably already crystallized \citep{kiley2020measuring-123, keskintürk2025what-483}. 

This study cracks open (if only just a bit) the black box of where such taste patterns may come from by studying a relatively young population of students---median age 18 years old---during their first two years of the transition to college at an elite private Catholic university in the United States (the University of Notre Dame). Leveraging a somewhat unique source of longitudinal survey data from the \textit{NetSense} study \citep{striegel2013lessons}, I model cultural taste trajectories across two cultural domains: private leisure reading choices and musical genre preferences, using a multivariate binary latent class growth model with time trends parameterized by quadratic growth curves. This approach allows me to model individual-level trajectories for each item simultaneously while estimating endogenous predictors of trajectory class membership. This approach helps to ascertain how sociodemographic background, pre-collegiate cultural capital, scholastic capital, secondary schooling habitus, and collegiate context structure individual assignments to distinct patterns of cultural choices.

The empirical analysis yields several key findings. First, rather than converging toward a uniform campus monoculture or drifting aimlessly, students in each cultural domain sort into four distinct cultural taste trajectories: Genre Specialists ($37.3\%$), Romance Readers ($23.4\%$), Fictionists ($22.9\%$), and Nonfictionists ($16.4\%$) in leisure reading; and Mainstreamers ($30.8\%$), Modern Rockers ($26.4\%$), Omnivores ($24.4\%$), and Classic Rockers ($18.4\%$) in musical taste. Second, trajectory dynamics display marked domain asymmetry: leisure reading exhibits selective winnowing and specialization as academic time scarcity mounts, whereas musical taste features substantial aesthetic expansion among Omnivores---who monotonically add country, folk, and Broadway show tunes---alongside consolidation among Mainstreamers. Third, multinomial logit models reveal that sorting into these trajectories is driven by pre-collegiate embodied cultural capital (prior arts event attendance and personal music library scale), scholastic capital (high school GPA and AP coursework), secondary schooling habitus (Catholic parochial school attendance), and gender identity, whereas parental household income and education have null direct predictive power. Finally, multilevel growth models confirm that these baseline attributes operate almost exclusively as sorting mechanisms into trajectory classes at matriculation---rather than moderating trajectory slopes.

Section~\ref{sec:lit} reviews theoretical perspectives on cultural capital, formative life-course transitions, and the omnivore-univore debate, extracting empirical expectations regarding trajectory morphology, domain asymmetry, and sorting mechanisms. Section~\ref{sec:data} introduces the \textit{NetSense} longitudinal panel, detailing the measurement of the nine leisure book types, the ten musical genres, and the baseline sociodemographic, scholastic, and cultural covariate blocks. Section~\ref{sec:methods} outlines the analytic strategy, describing the multivariate binary latent class growth model, the quadratic polynomial trajectory parameterization, the regularized endogenous multinomial logit model, and the multilevel specifications used to test trajectory slope moderation. Finally, Section~\ref{sec:discussion} synthesizes the core findings, addresses methodological limitations, and discusses the broader implications of these patterns for theories of the habitus-based socialization and the reproduction of cultural capital during emerging adulthood.

\section{Origins and Trajectories of Cultural Choice}\label{sec:lit}

\subsection{Structured Taste Trajectories}
The sociology of taste has long grappled with the tension between stability and change across the life course. In classical cultural reproduction theory, aesthetic dispositions are conceptualized as foundational elements of the habitus, inculcated early in the family of origin and remarkably durable over time \citep{bourdieu1984distinction,willekens2014family}. From this perspective, the transition to post-secondary education functions primarily to reinforce and legitimize pre-existing class dispositions, translating familial cultural endowments into institutionalized credentials. Yet emerging adulthood---the transitional stage spanning the late teens through the mid-twenties---represents a period of social reorganization characterized by residential independence, identity exploration, and shifting relational affiliations \citep{weinberger2017consuming,prieur2023distinctions}. Within collegiate environments, students are removed from direct parental oversight and embedded within dense peer networks, creating unique institutional opportunities for aesthetic experimentation and taste reconfiguration \citep{lewis2018conversion}. Moreover, empirical investigations of cultural socialization across the life course show that the direct influence of parental socialization steadily attenuates as individuals exit the parental home and transition into independent adulthood \citep{meuleman2018parental}.

Panel studies tracking cultural participation from adolescence into young adulthood offer critical empirical input to this debate. Examining Dutch panel cohorts, \citet{nagel2002participation} and \citet{nagel2010cultural} find that while parental cultural involvement anchors adolescent participation, progression through educational tracks increasingly structures cultural practices, producing persistent sorting rather than wholesale cultural mobility. Moreover, recent panel research examining intra-individual change in personal culture shows that cultural attitudes and foundational dispositions display striking temporal stability across adulthood, with systematic change occurring primarily during critical institutional transitions or through cohort replacement rather than random individual drift \citep{vaisey2016cultural-867,kiley2020measuring-123,vaisey2021model-based-deb}.

These empirical findings yield a primary expectation for collegiate taste trajectories: the transition to higher education should neither produce widespread aesthetic convergence toward a single institutional standard nor induce unpredictable individual churning. Instead, students should traverse distinct, bounded cultural taste trajectories. While baseline aesthetic commitments should exhibit durable continuity, trajectory movements should reflect structured adaptations to collegiate institutional rhythms and peer networks.

\subsection{Books versus Music}

Leisure book reading is a solitary, cognitive practice that demands sustained attention and substantial blocks of uninterrupted discretionary time \citep{kraaykamp2003literary,chalari2022adolescents}. In their panel investigation of reading behavior from adolescence to young adulthood, \citet{nagel2012reading} find that the transition to tertiary education does not lead to an overall increase in book-reading frequency. Instead, intense academic workloads and compulsory curricular reading generate acute time scarcity, displacing unassigned leisure reading. Under these constraints, readers cannot expand their repertoires indiscriminately; rather, they must make selective choices, concentrating their precious leisure reading time on preferred genres or abandoning leisure reading altogether \citep{kraaykamp2003literary, sokolova2020does-65c}. Furthermore, as \citet{meuleman2018parental} point out, reading practices are uniquely susceptible to educational and institutional shaping relative to other media, as schools and universities actively structure literary canons and reading workloads, leaving less room for the effect of direct family socialization. Consequently, leisure reading trajectories across collegiate semesters should be characterized by specialization and winnowing, as students either retreat to core genre preferences or experience secular declines in recreational reading.

In sharp contrast, musical genre preferences operate within an entirely different ecological and relational context \citep{lewis2018conversion-9be}. Music listening is mobile, ambient, and low-friction, effortlessly integrated into multitasking and daily routines \citep{webster2020taste,rimmer2010listening, denora2000music-0f0}. More importantly, musical preferences serve as a primary currency of identity performance and peer tie formation in collegiate life \citep{lewis2018conversion}. As \citet{weinberger2017consuming} argue, emerging adults view the consumption of novel experiential culture as an active accumulation of embodied cultural capital. Entering university exposes students to diverse regional and peer cultures, social scenes, and shared residential spaces, providing ideal conditions for cultural exploration \citep{gripsrud2011changing}. Longitudinal trends in musical taste further show that younger cohorts increasingly exhibit categorical tolerance and omnivorous consumption, adopting broader musical repertoires without abandoning core preferences \citep{lizardo2015musical-8c6,lizardo2016end-4fb, lizardo2026forward}.

Empirically, this domain divergence suggests that while book-reading trajectories will reflect winnowing and retreat under academic time pressure, musical-taste trajectories will feature substantial aesthetic expansion among eclectic students and consolidation around prominent contemporary genres for others.

\subsection{Relational Repertoires and Latent Typologies}

Since the formulation of the omnivore thesis \citep{peterson1992understanding-ce3,peterson1996changing}, sociological research has largely framed cultural differentiation along a unidimensional continuum spanning from univorous restriction (narrow, single-genre consumption) to omnivorous breadth (broad, eclectic consumption). Subsequent scholarship has linked omnivorous repertoires to educational attainment and social privilege \citep{warde2008omnivorous-e77,sullivan2007omnivore-018,vries2022what-1a2}. However, reducing cultural repertoires to summary variety counts masks critical qualitative distinctions in the symbolic composition of taste profiles \citep{goldberg2011mapping-77a, hazır2015cultural-992}.

As \citet{rimmer2012beyond} argues, the concept of a singular univore-to-omnivore axis obscures the internal logic and social meaning of specific genre combinations---what may be termed a ``musical habitus.'' An individual who likes classic rock, heavy metal, and jazz shares little aesthetic orientation with one who listens to commercial dance music, rap, and country, yet both may record the same number of variety counts on a survey checklist. Symbolic boundaries are drawn not merely through volume, but through relational pairings and patterned dislikes \citep{bryson1996anything-311,bryson1997what-933,sonnett2004musical-961,sonnett2016ambivalence-352}. Similarly, literary reading cannot be adequately represented by an aggregate index of books consumed. Reading preferences differentiate along distinct thematic axes---such as speculative fantasy, romantic narratives, or analytical non-fiction---that reflect divergent cognitive dispositions and aesthetic traditions \citep{kraaykamp2003literary,sokolova2020does-65c}.

Empirical studies using classification techniques illustrate the necessity of modeling taste repertoires as discrete configurations rather than linear scales \citep{thornton2024patterns,cicchelli2017aesthetico,spacek2017measuring}. In adolescent leisure, \citet{thornton2024patterns} find that participation clusters into distinct activity configurations with divergent longitudinal outcomes, while \citet{goldberg2016what-4e3} shows that spanning atypical genre boundaries conveys distinct symbolic meanings depending on which specific genres are bridged.

These arguments imply that collegiate cultural life cannot be captured by a single vertical hierarchy. Instead, taste repertoires should be modeled using approaches that can identify categorical typologies of consumers across both reading and musical genres, capturing unique combinations of genre engagement and selective avoidance.

\subsection{Embodied Capital, Institutional Habitus, and Family Origins}

What social mechanisms sort students into distinct cultural taste trajectories? In the classic Bourdieusian paradigm, cultural practices are primarily governed by family socioeconomic background, with parental education and economic resources determining an individual's cultural trajectory \citep{bourdieu1984distinction,willekens2014family}. Yet longitudinal evidence from panel studies reveals a more complex dynamic across the educational career. \citet{nagel2002participation} and \citet{nagel2010cultural} show that while parental cultural capital exerts strong effects during childhood, the direct predictive power of parental socioeconomic status weakens as students advance through the educational system. By the time students reach post-secondary education, educational tracking and scholastic achievement become the primary drivers of cultural participation. In a selective, residential university environment where students live apart from their families, parental income and parental schooling become more distant factors. In historical trend analyses, \citet{kraaykamp2016parental} find that traditional parental cultural capital (such as highbrow arts attendance) has progressively lost direct explanatory power for youth educational and cultural outcomes, while media socialization and active guidance have become increasingly important. Disentangling the mechanisms of intergenerational transmission, \citet{vanhek2015how} show that parents affect their children's cultural participation primarily through embodied modeling and active guidance during youth, with this influence operating indirectly through students' educational performance and scholastic achievements.

This structural decoupling is clarified by \citet{kraaykamp2010intergenerational}, who operationalize Bourdieu's threefold schema of cultural capital: embodied (active cultural participation), objectified (cultural possessions such as extensive book and music collections), and institutionalized (formal educational credentials). Their analysis shows that active cultural engagement in emerging adulthood depends directly on accumulated embodied habits and objectified cultural goods, while institutionalized credentials operate as a distinct, specialized axis of transmission. In residential collegiate contexts, embodied cultural capital accumulated prior to matriculation---such as early attendance at performing arts events and personal investments in media archives---acts as the direct vehicle through which cultural competence is enacted \citep{willekens2014family,spacek2017measuring}. Concurrently, scholastic capital, manifested in high school academic excellence and rigorous coursework, provides the cognitive resources and academic dispositions that facilitate engagement with complex or legitimate cultural forms \citep{lizardo2006cultural,wang2025cultural}.

Beyond familial and academic capital, secondary educational environments instill a distinctive institutional habitus. As \citet{khan2011privilege-68a} shows in his study of elite boarding school culture, secondary schooling institutions cultivate specific cultural styles, social ease, and tastes that prepare students for collegiate social life. Private parochial and Catholic preparatory schools, in particular, provide distinct extracurricular and normative climates that influence cultural engagement and peer affiliation independently of family income \citep{schmutz2016cultural}.

This leads to the empirical expectation that sorting into collegiate cultural taste trajectories will be driven primarily by embodied pre-collegiate cultural capital, scholastic achievement, and secondary schooling habitus, while parental income and education will exhibit attenuated direct influence.

\subsection{Gendered Symbolic Boundaries and Repertoire Allocation}

Cultural tastes are not gender-neutral; rather, aesthetic engagement is profoundly structured by gender socialization, identity performance, and peer conformity pressures \citep{schmutz2016cultural,lagaert2017engendering,nous2021multidimensional}. Throughout adolescence and emerging adulthood, cultural choices serve as visible markers through which individuals signal conformity to or divergence from normative gender roles.

In literature and the arts, empirical research consistently shows that women report higher levels of participation and broader cultural interests than men \citep{lizardo2006cultural,schmutz2016cultural}. Investigating the mechanisms underlying this disparity, \citet{lagaert2017engendering} show that gender conformity pressures act as powerful sorting mechanisms: adolescent girls experience social reinforcement for reading and literary engagement, whereas boys face peer policing that discourages highbrow expressive activities as incompatible with conventional masculinity. In reading choices, these dynamics channel women toward narrative-empathic genres, particularly fiction and romance, while steering men toward genre-based fiction, popular science, or non-fiction \citep{kraaykamp2003literary,chalari2022adolescents}.

In music, gendered boundaries are equally pronounced. Rock, heavy metal, and related guitar-driven genres have historically functioned as masculine-coded expressive fields, rooted in masculine peer networks and subcultural rituals \citep{rimmer2010listening,bryson1996anything-311}. By contrast, commercial pop, dance, and mainstream radio hits recruit broadly across gender lines but often serve as core elements of women's shared social gatherings.

These empirical precedents indicate that gender identity will function as a powerful sorting gate across both cultural domains. Specifically, women will be significantly more likely to enter fiction and romance reading trajectories and mainstream musical repertoires, while men will disproportionately populate rock subcultures and genre fiction.

\subsection{Extensive Versus Intensive Margins}

One methodological and substantive question in longitudinal cultural sociology is whether social background characteristics influence the \textit{rate of change} over time within a trajectory, or whether their influence is confined to \textit{initial sorting} into cultural taste trajectories.

Recent longitudinal research on personal culture provides crucial theoretical guidance. Applying panel models to identify enduring cultural dispositions, \citet{kiley2020measuring-123} and \citet{vaisey2021model-based-deb} show that individual cultural orientations are characterized by high baseline stability, with genuine intra-individual change operating within bounded, stable envelopes. When individuals transition into new institutional contexts, their social endowments, accumulated cultural capital, and gender identities govern which institutional niches and social networks they enter---an extensive sorting process \citep{mcpherson2004blau-9fe,lewis2018conversion}. Once an individual is settled into a particular cultural taste trajectory, however, subsequent trajectory movements are structured by collective collegiate rhythms, curriculum workloads, and domain-wide dynamics rather than idiosyncratic individual attributes.

This distinction between the extensive margin (latent class sorting) and the intensive margin (within-class trajectory slope moderation) implies a clear empirical expectation: baseline sociodemographic, scholastic, and cultural covariates will strongly predict membership in latent trajectory classes, but will display negligible interaction effects with time within those classes. Once sorted into a cultural taste trajectory, students experience common trajectory momentum regardless of background differences.

\section{Data and Measures}\label{sec:data}

\subsection{The NetSense Panel Study}

Empirical analyses use longitudinal survey microdata from the \textit{NetSense} study, a multi-wave panel study tracking a cohort of undergraduate students who matriculated at the University of Notre Dame in August 2011 \citep{striegel2013lessons}. \textit{NetSense} recruited an initial cohort of incoming first-year students, who were provided with sensor-enabled smartphones and surveyed at each semester boundary throughout their undergraduate careers. Surveys captured detailed social networks, academic performance, sociodemographic backgrounds, and extensive inventories of cultural practices. This paper uses the survey data on cultural practices, academic performance and goals, and socio-demographic background.

From the initial survey respondents, two students who had missing data across all survey waves on the cultural inventories were excluded, yielding an analytical cohort of $N = 201$ undergraduate students observed across a total of $1,006$ person-wave observations from freshman matriculation through junior year (Waves 1 through 6, Fall 2011 through Spring 2014). Importantly, cases are not listwise deleted across waves: the longitudinal panel is unbalanced, retaining all available semester observations per student (118 students observed across all 6 waves; 33 across 5 waves; 19 across 4 waves; 8 across 3 waves; 10 across 2 waves; and 13 across 1 wave). In the mixture modeling framework, repeated observations are grouped by student, allowing each individual's likelihood contribution to accumulate across their available measurement occasions under Full Information Maximum Likelihood (FIML) estimation.

\subsection{Longitudinal Cultural Tastes: Books and Music}

Across both cultural domains (music and books), items are measured as binary indicators ($Y_{itj} \in \{0, 1\}$) reflecting semester-level participation or aesthetic preference. For leisure book reading, tracked across Waves 1 through 6, reading choices were measured by survey question Q47: ``\textit{During the past semester, what types of books did you read for pleasure or leisure (not for class assignments)?}'' Binary indicators were recorded across nine literary categories: mysteries and detective fiction; thrillers and suspense; romance; science fiction and fantasy; general and literary fiction; history, politics, and current affairs; biography and memoir; self-help and personal development; and general non-fiction and popular science.

For musical taste preferences, also tracked across Waves 1 through 6, preferences were measured by survey question Q44: ``\textit{Which of the following types of music do you like to listen to?}'' From the full 22-genre inventory administered in the survey, the 10 most popular genres were selected for simultaneous multivariate trajectory modeling: rap and hip-hop; classic rock and oldies; rock and heavy metal; dance music; country; Broadway and show tunes; classical and chamber music; mood and easy listening; folk music; and jazz.

\subsection{Baseline Covariates}

To explain sorting into latent taste trajectories, we include 15 baseline predictors measured at freshman matriculation, organized into six conceptual blocks. First, demographic identity includes gender identity (1 = Woman, 0 = Man), racial identification (1 = White, 0 = Students of Color), and religious preference (1 = Roman Catholic, 0 = Non-Catholic). Second, family socioeconomic status includes parental household pretax annual income, converted from 14 survey brackets to category midpoints in thousands of dollars (ranging from \$5,000 to \$300,000), alongside parental educational attainment, measured as the maximum years of schooling completed between mother and father (ranging from 10 to 18 years).

Third, pre-collegiate scholastic capital includes high school academic achievement (1 = Mostly A or A- grades, 0 = B+ or lower), advanced degree aspirations (1 = Plans to pursue Master's, Ph.D., M.D., or J.D., 0 = Bachelor's degree only), and Advanced Placement coursework load (1 = Completed 5 or more AP courses, 0 = Under 5 AP courses or not offered). Fourth, secondary schooling habitus captures the institutional and social environment of secondary education, including attendance at a private Catholic or parochial high school (1 = Catholic/parochial, 0 = Public or secular prep) and attendance at a single-sex high school (1 = All-female or all-male academy, 0 = Co-educational).

Fifth, pre-collegiate cultural capital and aesthetic exposure captures embodied cultural practices established prior to university entry, including total arts and cultural event attendance during the year before college (summing across 12 event categories including theater, classical music, dance, museums, and historical sites, ranging from 0 to 12), pre-collegiate leisure reading volume (continuous count of non-assigned books read in the past year, ranging from 0 to 150), and large pre-collegiate music collection size (1 = Owned 1,000 or more tracks at matriculation, 0 = Under 1,000 tracks). Sixth, collegiate and geographic context encompasses declared undergraduate major (1 = Science, Technology, Engineering, or Mathematics, 0 = Arts, Humanities, Social Sciences, or Business) and hometown urbanicity, measured on an ordinal scale ranging from rural farm to major metropolitan area. Missing values in baseline covariates (each with < 5\% missingness) were imputed prior to modeling to preserve the full analytical cohort ($N = 201$). \section{Analytic Strategy}\label{sec:methods}

\subsection{Multivariate Binary Mixture Models}

To model cultural taste trajectories across collegiate life, we implement a multivariate binary latent class growth analysis (LCGA) within the finite mixture modeling framework \citep{grun2008flexmix}. Let $i \in \{1, \dots, N\}$ index undergraduate students ($N = 201$) observed across repeated collegiate semesters $t \in T_i \subseteq \{1, \dots, T\}$. At each semester wave, students report binary engagement ($Y_{itj} \in \{0, 1\}$) across an inventory of $J$ distinct cultural items ($J = 9$ for Leisure Book Reading; $J = 10$ for Musical Genre Preferences). We represent student $i$'s complete cultural response history across all observed waves and items as the matrix $\mathbf{Y}_i = \{Y_{itj}\}_{t \in T_i, j \in \{1, \dots, J\}}$.

Rather than forcing all students into a single aggregate trajectory, the finite mixture framework posits that the student population consists of $K$ unobserved latent classes, each defined by a distinct, class-specific trajectory profile across all $J$ items simultaneously. Conditioned on student $i$ belonging to latent class $k \in \{1, \dots, K\}$, item endorsements across categories and waves are modeled as conditionally independent Bernoulli trials governed by time-varying endorsement probabilities $\mu_{iktj} = P(Y_{itj} = 1 \mid c_i = k)$:
\begin{equation}
p(\mathbf{Y}_i \mid c_i = k) = \prod_{t \in T_i} \prod_{j=1}^{J} \mu_{iktj}^{Y_{itj}} (1 - \mu_{iktj})^{1 - Y_{itj}}
\end{equation}
The unconditional marginal likelihood for student $i$ sums over all $K$ latent classes weighted by their prior class probabilities $\pi_{ik} = P(c_i = k)$:
\begin{equation}
p(\mathbf{Y}_i) = \sum_{k=1}^K \pi_{ik} \prod_{t \in T_i} \prod_{j=1}^{J} \mu_{iktj}^{Y_{itj}} (1 - \mu_{iktj})^{1 - Y_{itj}}
\end{equation}
Crucially, repeated observations are grouped at the individual student level. This person-level likelihood accumulation ensures two important analytical properties. First, latent class assignments reflect cohesive, person-level taste trajectory typologies across emerging adulthood rather than fleeting semester-level fluctuations. Second, the product over $t \in T_i$ accommodates unbalanced longitudinal panels through Full Information Maximum Likelihood: students who missed a survey wave contribute full information for all semesters in which they participated ($1,006$ person-wave observations across $201$ students), completely avoiding the statistical inefficiency and potential bias of listwise panel deletion.

Conventional longitudinal growth curve modeling typically assumes either that all individuals follow a single average trajectory (with random individual variations around that average) or that a single summary index is used, such as the total number of genres liked or the total number of books read. However, summing across genres collapses qualitatively distinct cultural styles into an abstract number, obscuring the specific aesthetic boundaries that organize social life. In reality, cultural engagement is multidimensional: students do not simply consume ``more'' or ``less'' culture; they assemble specific, cohesive \textit{repertoires} or \textit{bundles} of cultural practices (such as pairing science fiction reading with classic rock and hip-hop listening).

The multivariate binary mixture model acts as an algorithmic pattern-finder across time and cultural items simultaneously. Rather than evaluating whether reading thrillers or listening to rap changes in isolation, the model examines the entire profile of binary checkmarks that each student provides each semester across all items. It asks: \textit{Are there distinct latent classes that group students who share similar combinations of tastes and similar trajectories of change over their collegiate career?}

Consider a concrete sociological example. Imagine two incoming first-year students, each of whom reports liking four musical genres at matriculation. A conventional unidimensional model would view these two students as identical in their cultural breadth. However, the multivariate binary mixture model looks beneath the summary score at the specific bundle of genres endorsed: the first student may listen to rap/hip-hop, dance music, classic rock, and country---a commercial mainstream portfolio. The second student may listen to classical music, jazz, folk, and classic rock---an eclectic, highbrow-leaning portfolio. Over the subsequent six semesters, the model tracks how each student's entire portfolio evolves. It can identify that the first student follows a cultural taste trajectory in which rock preferences winnow while country surges to near-universal adoption, while the second student follows an omnivorous trajectory in which cross-genre diversity is preserved throughout college. By evaluating all items jointly, the model captures cultural lifestyles as integrated taste systems rather than disconnected, independent choices.

\subsection{Flexible Quadratic Polynomial Growth Parameterization}

Because empirical inspection of semester-by-semester taste changes reveals smooth, gradual evolutionary patterns rather than abrupt or high-frequency fluctuations, the probability of endorsing item $j$ at time $t$ in class $k$ is parameterized via an inverse logit link using linear and quadratic orthogonalized temporal polynomial terms:
\begin{equation}
\text{logit}(\mu_{iktj}) = \beta_{0kj} + \beta_{1kj} \text{Time}_{it} + \beta_{2kj} \text{Time}_{it}^2
\end{equation}
where $\text{Time}_{it} = \text{Wave}_{it} - 1 \in \{0, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5\}$ centers time at collegiate matriculation ($t = 0$), $\beta_{1kj}$ captures the initial growth velocity at college entry, and $\beta_{2kj}$ captures non-linear acceleration, deceleration, or mid-career curvature.

Adopting a quadratic polynomial parameterization provides substantial methodological advantages over higher-order spline or cubic specifications. First, it ensures strict model parsimony: by estimating three item-level growth parameters (intercept, linear slope, and quadratic curvature) rather than four, it reduces the item-level parameter space by 25\% (saving 9 free parameters per class in books and 10 in music). Second, it avoids overfitting to semester-specific measurement churn while retaining full flexibility to detect monotonic expansions, gradual winnowing, and mid-collegiate inflections. Third, standard quadratic coefficients offer straightforward substantive interpretability, linking growth parameters directly to sociological concepts of taste acceleration and deceleration across emerging adulthood.

\subsection{Endogenous Multinomial Concomitant Models}

To avoid the classification error and attenuation inherent in two-step estimation, latent class membership probabilities $\pi_{ik} = P(c_i = k \mid \mathbf{X}_i)$ are estimated jointly with item trajectories through an endogenous multinomial logit model:

\begin{equation}
\pi_{ik} = \frac{\exp(\mathbf{X}_i \boldsymbol{\gamma}_k)}{\sum_{l=1}^K \exp(\mathbf{X}_i \boldsymbol{\gamma}_l)}
\end{equation}

where $\boldsymbol{\gamma}_1 = \mathbf{0}$ sets Class 1 as the reference baseline. Across both cultural domains, the expanded battery of 15 baseline predictors is evaluated across six theoretical blocks: demographic identity (gender, race, religion); family socioeconomic status (parental household income and parental education); pre-collegiate scholastic capital (high school academic performance, advanced degree aspirations, and high AP load); secondary schooling habitus (Catholic parochial high school and single-sex schooling); pre-collegiate cultural capital and aesthetic exposure (prior arts event attendance, prior leisure reading volume, and large pre-collegiate music collection); and collegiate and geographic context (declared STEM major and hometown urbanicity).

\subsection{Model-Implied Marginal Class Probabilities}

While Table~\ref{tab:blocks} evaluates the overall predictive power of theoretical variable blocks, interpreting individual coefficients from multinomial logit models is often complicated by arbitrary reference baselines and non-linear log-odds metrics. Table~\ref{tab:app_coefs} in the Appendix reports the complete multivariable parameter estimates and odds ratios for all 15 baseline covariates across both domains.\footnote{To address sparse-cell and quasi-complete separation issues inherent in categorical choice models with specialized cultural subcultures, multivariable parameters were estimated using penalized maximum likelihood with a mild weight-decay regularization penalty ($\lambda = 0.05$) via the \texttt{nnet} package \citep{venables2002modern} in \textsf{R} \citep{Rmanual}. Specifically, in the leisure reading domain, Romance Readers contains only a single male student ($n = 46$ women, $1$ man out of $47$), and Nonfictionists contains zero students without advanced degree aspirations ($n = 33$ aspirants, $0$ non-aspirants). Similarly, in musical preferences, Classic Rockers contains zero students without advanced degree aspirations ($n = 37$ aspirants, $0$ non-aspirants). Under standard unpenalized maximum likelihood estimation, zero and near-zero cell counts produce quasi-complete separation \citep{albert1984existence,firth1993bias}, driving the corresponding logit parameters toward the parameter space boundary ($\beta \to \pm\infty$), inflating odds ratios to astronomical magnitudes ($\text{OR} > 10^8$), and generating ill-conditioned covariance matrices with artificially inflated standard errors. Introducing an $L_2$ regularization penalty stabilizes the likelihood surface, preventing numerical divergence while preserving the substantive direction, relative magnitude, and empirical ranking of effects. This penalization ensures that the parameter covariance matrix remains strictly positive definite, yielding well-calibrated, finite simulation confidence intervals and model-implied marginal predicted class probabilities.} Following recent methodological practice in latent growth mixture analysis, we translate these multivariable model parameters into model-implied marginal predicted class probabilities with 95\% simulation confidence intervals, holding other background covariates constant at sample means and reference categories. Rather than indicating that family cultural reproduction has ceased to matter in emerging adulthood, the lack of explanatory power for parental socioeconomic status and cultural capital is likely a consequence of the highly selected nature of the sample. Matriculating at a selective, elite private research university acts as an upstream institutional filter that homogenizes student family backgrounds around high levels of economic and cultural capital. By truncating the variance of parental income and parental education relative to the general population, this institutional selection mechanism attenuates the observed statistical associations between family origin and collegiate cultural sorting, allowing proximate individual characteristics---such as prior academic performance and field of study---to dominate observed taste differentiation on campus.\footnote{Demographic characteristics operate almost exclusively as between-class sorting gates (the extensive margin) rather than within-class slope modifiers (the intensive margin). \section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Summary of Key Results}

This study implemented a finite mixture modeling framework, parameterized with quadratic polynomial growth curves and endogenous multinomial concomitants, to examine longitudinal cultural taste trajectories across emerging adulthood in the \textit{NetSense} study. By estimating item-specific growth parameters across 9 book reading genres and 10 musical genres across all six collegiate semesters ($N = 201$, $1,006$ person-wave observations), this analysis yields four primary empirical findings that speak directly to debates on cultural stratification, life course transitions, and higher education.

First, item-level trajectory modeling shows that collegiate cultural life does not converge toward a homogeneous campus taste culture, nor does it undergo erratic aesthetic drift. Instead, undergraduates divide into four distinct, persistent cultural taste trajectories in each expressive sphere. In leisure reading, students sort into Genre Specialists ($37.3\%$), who concentrate heavily in speculative fiction, other fiction, and thrillers while avoiding romance; Romance Readers ($23.4\%$), who combine romance and general fiction; Fictionists ($22.9\%$), who read broadly across all fiction genres; and Nonfictionists ($16.4\%$), who focus on biography, history, and politics. In musical taste, students sort into Mainstreamers ($30.8\%$), who focus strictly on commercial radio hits; Modern Rockers ($26.4\%$), who combine rock and heavy metal with contemporary hip-hop and dance music; Omnivores ($24.4\%$), who maintain high preference probabilities across all ten genres simultaneously; and Classic Rockers ($18.4\%$), who pair classic rock with traditional highbrow forms such as jazz, classical music, and Broadway show tunes. Parameterizing trajectories through linear and quadratic terms shows that these taste orientations evolve through smooth, gradual adjustments rather than abrupt shocks or high-frequency churn.

Second, the analysis uncovers a pronounced domain asymmetry in the evolution of cultural repertoires across the college career. In leisure book reading, taste change is overwhelmingly characterized by specialization and winnowing: members of all four latent classes lose tastes across the collegiate life course, and when tastes are gained, the increases are slight and highly targeted. In contrast, musical taste trajectories feature substantial aesthetic expansion alongside selective contraction. For every musical class except Mainstreamers, students gain almost as many musical tastes as they shed. Most notably, Omnivores reinforce their expansive profile over time by monotonically adopting country music (+24.0\%), folk music (+11.8\%), and Broadway show tunes (+10.6\%), while Mainstreamers become more univorous by dropping classic rock (-22.5\%) and dance music (-26.1\%). This asymmetry indicates that private, solitary practices such as leisure reading face institutional time pressures that induce specialization, whereas socially shared media like music provide versatile cultural currency for collegiate peer affiliation.

Third, class-stratified multilevel growth curve models confirm an essential distinction between the extensive and intensive margins of cultural stratification. Baseline sociodemographic characteristics, pre-collegiate habitus, and undergraduate field of study operate almost exclusively as between-class sorting gates (the extensive margin) that allocate students into broad cultural taste trajectories at matriculation. Once students enter a specific trajectory, however, within-class slope moderation by these background attributes is statistically null ($p > 0.10$). Sociodemographic background dictates which cultural trajectory a student enters, but does not alter the velocity or temporal curvature of taste change within that trajectory.

Fourth, endogenous multinomial concomitant models show that sorting into these cultural taste trajectories is driven by pre-collegiate cultural capital, scholastic capital, secondary schooling habitus, and gender identity rather than by inherited family socioeconomic resources. Pre-collegiate cultural capital serves as a primary sorting gateway into musical omnivorousness: students arriving with extensive arts exposure (attending dramatic plays, musical theater, and visiting historical landmarks) are overwhelmingly channeled into omnivorous trajectories (38.1\% vs.\ 13.3\% for low exposure), while cutting the probability of entering modern rock and mainstream trajectories. Similarly, owning a large personal music library at matriculation cuts the odds of falling into commercial mainstream univorousness by 75\% ($\text{OR} = 0.25, p = 0.005$). Concurrently, scholastic capital (high school GPA and AP coursework) and secondary schooling habitus act as decisive institutional sorting mechanisms: completing five or more AP courses elevates the odds of entering the Omnivore trajectory five-fold ($\text{OR} = 5.00, p < 0.01$), while attending a private Catholic parochial high school substantially increases the odds of sorting into avid fiction-reading trajectories ($\text{OR} = 3.90, p = 0.015$). Meanwhile, gender identity remains the single strongest structural differentiator across both domains, channeling women into Romance reading (59.8\% vs.\ 1.0\% for men) and mainstream music (49.8\% vs.\ 20.8\%), while men concentrate in speculative fiction (64.4\% vs.\ 10.5\%) and rock subcultures.

Crucially, throughout all specifications, parental socioeconomic status (parental household income and parental education) displays negligible direct sorting power ($p > 0.45$ across domains). Rather than showing that social origins no longer matter, this empirical pattern highlights the upstream filtering role of elite university admissions, which restricts the variance of family wealth and elevates acquired attributes---specifically pre-collegiate cultural exposure, secondary schooling habitus, and scholastic capital---as the active channels of collegiate cultural reproduction. Together, these results provide a nuanced foundation for considering the methodological caveats and institutional implications that follow.

\subsection{Limitations and Suggestions for Future Work}

Several considerations should guide the interpretation of these findings. First, regarding operationalization and measurement constraints, the self-reported binary batteries analyzed here record whether an individual engaged in a cultural activity or endorsed a musical genre during a given semester, but do not capture the intensive margin of engagement, such as the repeated frequency of attendance, financial expenditures, or the subjective depth of aesthetic experience. Furthermore, discrete-item batteries are constrained by fixed survey inventories that omit emerging, decentralized digital cultural forms. Future research would benefit from pairing longitudinal survey instruments with multimodal digital trace data, such as streaming platform logs, electronic ticketing records, or mobile reading tracking applications, to capture the continuous volume and affective valence of cultural encounters.

Second, regarding threats to causal inference and contextual confounding, this observational design models endogenous sorting into cultural taste trajectories rather than exogenous causal interventions. However, by directly incorporating pre-collegiate cultural capital (prior arts event attendance, leisure reading volume, and music library size) alongside secondary schooling habitus and scholastic capital at matriculation, this specification substantially tightens causal inference by controlling for pre-existing behavioral momentum. Unmeasured contextual factors, such as campus-subsidized cultural excursions, department-specific course reading requirements, or variations in financial aid packages that alter disposable leisure income, could still influence within-college taste evolution. Future studies could leverage natural experiments, such as randomized housing assignments or lottery-based course enrollments, to isolate institutional treatment effects from pre-existing student self-selection.

Third, regarding directionality, selection, and reciprocal feedback loops, cultural taste trajectories likely co-evolve alongside interpersonal social networks and collegiate friendship circles. Students may select peers who share similar aesthetic preferences, while peer networks simultaneously reinforce or broaden existing cultural repertoires through social contagion. This person-level latent class formulation treats class membership as an enduring person-level type rather than modeling bidirectional dynamic feedback over time. Future investigations should integrate multivariate mixture models with continuous-time stochastic actor-oriented models or cross-lagged panel models to unpack how peer tie formation and taste diversification dynamically influence each other across the collegiate life course.

Fourth, regarding temporal granularity and panel attrition, the NetSense panel tracks participants across semester-length intervals over three full collegiate years. While semester-spaced waves effectively capture macro-level institutional rhythms, such as transitions between academic years and major declarations, they cannot detect short-term micro-churn in media consumption or fleeting aesthetic fads. Moreover, although the analytic samples were restricted to respondents with high longitudinal compliance, panel studies over multiple collegiate years remain vulnerable to selective attrition, particularly among students experiencing academic distress or social isolation. Implementing ecological momentary assessment (EMA) or frequent micro-surveys would enable researchers to observe micro-level taste shifts and track non-response dynamics over extended developmental windows.

Fifth, regarding institutional scope and generalizability, the NetSense cohort is situated at a single selective, private, highly residential research university with a distinctive collegiate culture and a predominantly White (67.8\%) and high-SES student body. The institutional opportunity structures present on this campus---including active campus performing arts centers, subsidized student programming, and a strong residential hall system---differ markedly from those found in large public institutions, commuter campuses, or community colleges. 

Crucially, the highly selected nature of this elite undergraduate sample provides the most plausible explanation for the observed lack of explanatory power for parental socioeconomic background. Highly selective university admissions serve as an upstream institutional filter that disproportionately admits students from affluent, highly educated households with high baseline endowments of economic and cultural capital. By severely compressing the variance of parental income and parental education relative to the general population, this institutional selection mechanism creates a restriction-of-range problem that attenuates the observed statistical associations between family origin and collegiate cultural sorting. 

Rather than showing that class reproduction has dissolved in emerging adulthood, these findings show that once students pass through the elite admissions threshold, proximate embodied factors---specifically pre-collegiate cultural capital, secondary schooling habitus, and pre-collegiate scholastic capital---supersede abstract family wealth as the primary visible sorting channels. Comparative research across diverse organizational settings, regional ecologies, and less selective higher education institutions will be essential to test whether parental socioeconomic status re-emerges as a decisive sorting mechanism in more socioeconomically heterogeneous student bodies.

\subsection{Implications: Repertoires, Institutions, and the Collegiate Habitus}

These empirical findings contribute directly to sociological debates regarding cultural capital, the structure of aesthetic repertoires, and life-course transitions. By returning to the theoretical tensions articulated at the outset, the longitudinal analysis clarifies how taste patterns originate, diversify, and crystallize during emerging adulthood.

First, the results enrich the debate over the intergenerational transmission of cultural capital by empirically affirming the threefold schema articulated by \citet{kraaykamp2010intergenerational}. In their analysis of Bourdieusian reproduction, \citet{kraaykamp2010intergenerational} show that embodied cultural participation and objectified cultural possessions constitute distinct empirical states from institutionalized credentials. Our findings show this principle in action within higher education: sorting into collegiate cultural taste trajectories is driven not by distal parental socioeconomic status, but by proximate embodied cultural capital (prior attendance at arts events) and objectified cultural assets (accumulated music libraries and leisure book collections) acquired prior to matriculation. This finding directly aligns with \citet{vanhek2015how}, who show that parental cultural influence operates primarily through active guidance and socialization during childhood rather than passive status inheritance, channeling children into educational achievement and embodied competence. Furthermore, consistent with historical trend analyses by \citet{kraaykamp2016parental}, traditional parental cultural capital has progressively lost direct sorting power in contemporary cohorts, yielding to active media socialization and academic capital as the effective drivers of taste differentiation.

Second, our findings substantiate life-course theories of emerging adulthood as a period of structured institutional transition. As \citet{meuleman2018parental}, \citet{nagel2002participation}, and \citet{nagel2010cultural} argue, the direct grip of parental socialization steadily weakens as young people leave the parental household and enter higher education. Rather than observing unconstrained aesthetic drift, however, the persistence of four distinct trajectory classes in each domain confirms the core proposition of recent panel research on personal culture: individual dispositions remain remarkably stable across emerging adulthood, exhibiting bounded adaptations to collegiate schedules rather than volatile conversion \citep{vaisey2016cultural-867,kiley2020measuring-123,vaisey2021model-based-deb}. Collegiate life serves as an arena of ``exploratory experience consumption'' \citep{weinberger2017consuming}, but this exploration is channeled along pre-existing dispositional tracks.

Third, the marked domain asymmetry uncovered between book reading and musical taste speaks directly to the material and ecological constraints governing cultural practices. In leisure reading, the ubiquitous pattern of winnowing and specialization reflects the severe time scarcity documented in panel studies of educational transitions \citep{nagel2012reading,kraaykamp2003literary}. Because book reading requires sustained cognitive focus and uninterrupted leisure, mounting academic workloads displace unassigned reading, forcing students to concentrate on core genres or retreat from reading altogether \citep{chalari2022adolescents,sokolova2020does-65c}. Moreover, as \citet{meuleman2018parental} observe, reading is uniquely subordinated to formal schooling requirements, diminishing the scope for spontaneous leisure expansion. Musical taste, by contrast, operates under the low-friction, ambient affordances of mobile digital listening \citep{webster2020taste,denora2000music-0f0}. Because music listening serves as an indispensable medium for peer signaling and social network tie formation on campus \citep{lewis2018conversion,lewis2018conversion-9be}, musical omnivores actively broaden their repertoires over time, incorporating country, folk, and musical theater as versatile interactional currency \citep{lizardo2015musical-8c6,lizardo2026forward}.

Fourth, our findings provide compelling longitudinal support for relational critiques of the univore-omnivore hierarchy \citep{rimmer2011beyond,rimmer2012beyond,goldberg2011mapping-77a}. Rather than distributing along a unidimensional continuum of cultural breadth \citep{peterson1996changing-967,warde2008omnivorous-e77,sullivan2007omnivore-018,vries2022what-1a2}, undergraduates organize their tastes into cohesive relational bundles characterized by distinct genre configurations and patterned dislikes \citep{bryson1996anything-311,bryson1997what-933,sonnett2004musical-961,sonnett2016ambivalence-352}. Classic Rockers combine rock with traditional highbrow genres (classical, jazz, Broadway) while rejecting country and commercial dance music; Modern Rockers blend rock with hip-hop and dance music while rejecting highbrow forms; and Mainstreamers embrace radio pop while rejecting rock entirely. These qualitative configurations confirm that cultural omnivorousness is not mere indiscriminate consumption, but a specific, cultivated ``musical habitus'' that spans selected genres while maintaining symbolic boundaries \citep{rimmer2012beyond,goldberg2016what-4e3}.

Fifth, the enduring sorting power of gender identity across both domains underscores how aesthetic repertoires remain deeply gendered through social conformity pressures \citep{lagaert2017engendering,schmutz2016cultural,nous2021multidimensional}. In reading, women disproportionately enter romance and fiction trajectories, while men sort into genre fiction and speculative literature \citep{kraaykamp2003literary}; in music, men heavily populate guitar-driven rock subcultures, while women sort into mainstream pop and dance repertoires \citep{rimmer2010listening}. These durable divisions indicate that collegiate social environments do not dissolve gendered cultural expectations, but rather reinforce them through peer interaction.

Finally, the sharp empirical divergence between extensive-margin sorting and intensive-margin slope stability provides a vital theoretical lesson for sociology: the collegiate habitus acts as an institutional sorting machine rather than a transformative conversion chamber. By sorting students into distinct cultural taste trajectories at matriculation based on accumulated scholastic capital, secondary schooling habitus \citep{khan2011privilege-68a,schmutz2016cultural,wang2025cultural}, and embodied cultural capital, elite universities preserve and amplify pre-collegiate distinctions. Once students enter a given trajectory, collective collegiate rhythms govern the tempo of change, producing uniform trajectory momentum across diverse demographic backgrounds. Recognizing this decoupling of extensive sorting from intensive change provides a clearer foundation for understanding how cultural inequality is reproduced across the transition to higher education.
